# The Ancient Viking World — A Guide to Viking Age Culture

*By Yrsa Freydisdottir*

---

## Introduction

This guide is about the world the Vikings actually lived in: the farms and harbours, laws and feuds, ships and graves of Scandinavia and its far-flung settlements in the early Middle Ages. It is built on academic knowledge — archaeology first of all, supported by runic inscriptions, place names, contemporary foreign accounts, and the great body of Old Norse literature written down, mostly in Iceland, centuries after the events it remembers.

That evidence base matters, because the Viking Age is unusually easy to mythologise. Almost everything about it arrives filtered: through monks who were being raided, through saga authors writing two hundred years later, through nineteenth-century national romanticism, and through modern entertainment. Throughout this guide, claims are graded honestly. Things dug out of the ground are stated as finds. Things a text says are attributed to that text. Things scholars argue about are presented as arguments. Things nobody actually knows are admitted to be unknown.

One discipline runs through everything that follows: the people of this age did not call themselves "the Vikings," and most of them never went raiding in their lives. What we call Viking culture is, for the great majority, the culture of early medieval Scandinavian farmers, fishers, traders, craftspeople, and slaves — among whom a minority, for roughly two and a half centuries, also went *a-viking*.

## 1. Who the "Vikings" Were

### An activity, not an ethnicity

The Old Norse word *víkingr* means a sea-raider, a pirate — someone who goes out on an expedition for plunder. The feminine noun *víking* names the activity itself. The verb phrase was "to go a-viking" (*fara í víking*). Crucially, *víkingr* was not an ethnic label. A man from Hordaland in Norway or from Jutland in Denmark was a Norwegian or a Dane; he was a *víkingr* only while, and insofar as, he was raiding. The word could even carry a negative charge in the sources — a *víkingr* in a saga is sometimes simply a robber who ambushes travellers, not a hero.

The peoples involved were the Norse-speaking populations of early medieval Scandinavia:

- **Danes**, in modern Denmark and Scania (then Danish), who dominated the southern Baltic approaches and turned most forcefully toward England and Francia.
- **Norwegians**, along the long Atlantic-facing coast of modern Norway, whose sea-roads ran west — to Shetland, Orkney, the Hebrides, Ireland, Iceland, and beyond.
- **Swedes**, around Lake Mälaren and the Baltic coasts, whose expeditions ran predominantly east, down the rivers of what is now Russia and Ukraine toward Byzantium and the Islamic world.
- **Geats** (the people of Götaland, remembered in *Beowulf*) and **Gotlanders**, the islanders of Gotland, who sat at a Baltic crossroads, farmed, traded, and raised the richest concentration of picture stones in the North.

These were not unified nations. They were dozens of small kingdoms, chiefdoms, and free farming districts sharing a language (Old Norse), a pantheon, a law tradition, and a seafaring technology — and frequently fighting each other.

### The dates, and why they are conventions

The conventional Viking Age runs from **793 to 1066**. Both dates are bookends chosen by historians, not walls in time.

**793** is the year Norse raiders sacked the monastery of Lindisfarne, off the Northumbrian coast — an event recorded with horror in the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* and in Alcuin's letters. It is a convenient start because it is dramatic and documented, but Norse raiding demonstrably predates it: in about 789, three ships from Hordaland landed at Portland in Dorset and killed a royal reeve, an incident the Chronicle itself records. Scandinavian trade, migration, and seafaring reach back far earlier still, and the first raids were a change of degree and direction, not a sudden invention.

**1066** is the year Harald Hardrada of Norway was defeated and killed at Stamford Bridge in Yorkshire by Harold Godwinson — who was himself killed by William of Normandy at Hastings three weeks later. Stamford Bridge is a tidy end-point for Scandinavian attempts to conquer England, but Scandinavian raiding, settlement, and kingship did not stop on that day; Norse-Gaelic Dublin, the earldom of Orkney, and Scandinavian influence across the North Atlantic continued for centuries.

### The roots: the Vendel period

Scholars therefore increasingly stress the **Vendel period** (roughly 550–790 in Sweden, part of the wider Migration and Merovingian eras) as the Viking Age's foundation. In the rich boat graves of Vendel and Valsgärde in Uppland lie the direct ancestors of Viking Age ship burial, weapon styles, and aristocratic culture, a full two centuries before Lindisfarne. The Viking Age did not erupt from nowhere; it was what happened when an old seafaring, gift-giving, kin-based society met richer targets, better sails, population pressure on good farmland, and the silver economies of Francia, England, and the Caliphate.

## 2. The Lands

### Scandinavia itself

Geography did much of the Vikings' thinking for them. Norway is a mountain wall along the Atlantic: arable land is scarce, squeezed into valley bottoms and coastal strips, but the sea — sheltered by the skerry guard of offshore islands, warmed by the North Atlantic drift — is a highway. Denmark is the opposite: low, flat, fertile, a land bridge and sea-gate between the Baltic and the North Sea, rich in farmland and perfectly placed to tax trade. Sweden's heartland lay inland around the Mälaren basin, with long Baltic coasts facing east. Everywhere, winters were long, the growing season short, and survival depended on storing summer's surplus — hay for livestock above all — and on the sea's fish. A society so placed will produce sailors the way a steppe produces horsemen.

### The diaspora

What makes the age extraordinary is how far this small population reached.

**The North Atlantic stepping-stones.** Norse settlers took the **Faroe Islands** (traditionally in the early ninth century, with some evidence of earlier Irish monks — the *papar* the Icelandic sources remember) and then **Iceland**, in the settlement period (*landnám*) of roughly 870–930. *Landnámabók*, the Book of Settlements, names just over 400 primary settlers and thousands of dependents, and Icelanders preserved settlement genealogies with obsessive care. Iceland had no native population — the only place the Norse settled empty land — and within two generations some 60,000 people may have lived there (estimates vary).

**Greenland and Vinland.** Around 985, Erik the Red, exiled from Iceland, led settlers to Greenland's south-western fjords, founding the Eastern and Western Settlements — ultimately a few thousand Norse Greenlanders farming at the edge of the habitable world for nearly five centuries. From Greenland, around the year 1000, expeditions reached North America: Bjarni Herjólfsson sighted land after being blown off course, and Leif Erikson and others wintered on the coast the sagas call **Vinland**. Archaeology settled the saga debate: at **L'Anse aux Meadows** in Newfoundland, excavated in the 1960s, lie the remains of Norse buildings, ironworking slag, and a bronze ringed pin — the only confirmed Norse site in the Americas, a small base camp of perhaps a few dozen people, occupied around 1000 (tree-ring evidence published in 2021 dates Norse wood-cutting there to exactly 1021). The settlements in America did not last; contact with Indigenous peoples, distance, and economics saw to that.

**The Danelaw.** In England, the Great Heathen Army of 865 onwards conquered and settled Northumbria, East Anglia, and much of Mercia. The resulting **Danelaw** — the region under Danish law — left a permanent deposit in English: hundreds of place names in *-by*, *-thorpe*, and *-thwaite*, and everyday words from *sky* and *egg* to *they* and *law* itself (an Old Norse loan). Scandinavian settlers did not merely raid England; they farmed it, married into it, and were absorbed by it within generations.

**Normandy.** In 911 the Frankish king Charles the Simple granted the Viking leader Rollo land around Rouen — the seed of **Normandy**, "the land of the Northmen" — in return for defence against other Vikings. Within three generations Rollo's descendants spoke French, rode as Frankish cavalry, and built Romanesque cathedrals; within five, Rollo's descendant William conquered England. It is the sharpest illustration of how quickly Norse settlers could become something else.

**The Rus' and the rivers of the east.** Swedish adventurers — called *Rus'* in the sources — moved down the great river systems: the Volga, the Dnieper, portaging between watersheds, founding or capturing trading centres and contributing to the polity centred on Kyiv. Their roads reached **Constantinople** (Miklagarðr, "the Great City"), where Norse mercenaries formed the Byzantine emperor's Varangian Guard, and **Baghdad's** wider Islamic world. The proof is in the ground: tens of thousands of Islamic silver **dirhams** have been found in Scandinavian hoards, above all on Gotland — coins minted in Samarkand and Baghdad, carried north in exchange for furs, slaves, wax, and amber. A Gotlandic farmer's buried hoard is the far end of a trade chain that began on the Silk Road.

## 3. Society

### Kin first: the ætt

The basic unit of Viking Age society was not the individual or the state but the **kin group** — the *ætt*. Your kin determined your legal standing, your marriage prospects, your duty of vengeance, and your right to compensation. To be kinless was to be almost a non-person; the worst fate in the moral imagination of the sagas is exile, severed from the network that makes a human life defensible. Identity was worn in the name itself: a person was their father's (or sometimes mother's) son or daughter — Bjorn Thordarson, Gudrid Thorbjarnardóttir — and more than a thousand years later, Icelanders still are.

### Jarl, karl, þrall: the mythic charter and the messy reality

The eddic poem *Rígsþula* tells how the god Rígr fathered the three estates of mankind: **þrall** (slave), born to labour; **karl** (free farmer), born to work the land; and **jarl** (earl), born to rule and make war. It is the only Norse myth of social origins, and it presents hierarchy as cosmically ordained — a *mythic charter* for the social order.

Reality was messier and more mobile. The jarl class shade into local chieftains (*hǫfðingjar*) and, in Iceland, the *goðar* (singular *goði*) — chieftain-priests whose authority rested on a transferable office (*goðorð*) and on the loyalty of their thingmen, not on landownership alone. The karl class ranged from wealthy landowning farmers (*bœndr*), who were the real political weight of the þing, down to tenants and hired hands. And the boundary around slavery, though brutal, was not always permanent, as we will see. Over the Viking Age, moreover, the map of power consolidated: petty kingdoms merged, and by the eleventh century recognisable kingdoms of Norway, Denmark, and Sweden were emerging — Harald Fairhair's unification of Norway (traditionally c. 872), Harald Bluetooth's Denmark, and the slower Swedish coalescence. Kingship grew by taxing trade, controlling mints and towns, and finally by wielding the Church as an administrative machine.

### Law and the þing

Scandinavians governed themselves, where kings were weak or absent, through the **þing** — the assembly. Free men gathered at local and regional things to settle disputes, proclaim laws, witness marriages and adoptions, and judge cases. Iceland, which had no king at all in its Commonwealth period (930–1262), built the most elaborate version: the **Alþingi**, meeting each summer at Þingvellir, with a **law-speaker** (*lǫgsǫgumaðr*) who recited the law aloud over a three-year cycle, and courts including the notoriously technical Fifth Court. Iceland's laws were eventually written down as **Grágás**, the "Grey Goose" code, in manuscripts of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries.

The system's sanctions were characteristic. Much conflict ended not in punishment but in **compensation** — negotiated payments for killings and injuries, scaled by the victim's status, functionally akin to the wergild of Anglo-Saxon law. Its heaviest weapons were the forms of **outlawry**: full outlawry (*skóggangr*) stripped a person of all legal protection — anyone could kill them without penalty, and aiding them was itself an offence — while lesser outlawry imposed three years' exile. In a kin society, outlawry worked because legal existence *was* social existence.

### Honour, feud, and reconciliation

Honour (*virðing*) was a public currency: a person's and a family's worth in others' eyes, defended by deed and word. Insults, seductions, and killings created debts of honour that could ignite **feud** — the chain of retaliatory killings that drives so many sagas. But the sagas are equally full of the machinery that *stopped* feuds: arbitration by respected men, marriage alliances, fosterage, and compensation settlements. Historians stress that feud was a regulated process with off-ramps, not mere anarchy — and that the sagas, written later by a Christianised, more state-ordered society, may exaggerate the violence of the age they mourn.

### Slavery, honestly

Slavery was real, widespread, and foundational, and any honest guide must say so plainly. **Þrælar** (slaves) were property. They were captured in raids (the Dublin slave market was among the largest in western Europe), born to enslaved mothers, inherited, traded, and in some early accounts sacrificed at their owners' funerals — Ibn Fadlan's description of a Rus funeral includes the killing of a slave woman, presented in Section 4 below as what it is: one observer's account. Slaves worked farms, hauled, herded, and ground grain; the exact proportion of the population enslaved is unknowable, but historians generally treat slave labour as a significant strand of the economy, especially on larger farms, and the slave trade as a major driver of raiding economics — captives were a primary export. Manumission existed: freed slaves (*leysingjar*) gained a limited freedom, with obligations to their former owners persisting for a generation or two. Some place names and a famous runestone or two record freedpeople who rose. Slavery faded across the twelfth and thirteenth centuries as the Church discouraged the enslavement of Christians and the economics of raiding died — but it faded slowly, and its victims are the Viking Age's largest forgotten population.

## 4. Daily Life

### The longhouse

Home, for most, was the **longhouse**: a long, low, slightly boat-curved building of timber, turf, or stone, with a central hearth running down the middle, smoke escaping through a roof vent, benches along the walls for sitting and sleeping, and byres at one end in colder regions so the animals' heat warmed the household. Turf roofs and thick walls fought the winter; inside it was dark, smoky, and crowded with family, servants, and slaves together. Great chieftains built halls of saga scale — the excavated longhouse at Borg in Lofoten is 83 metres long — but the ordinary farm was a cluster of smaller buildings: dwelling, byre, barn, forge, storehouse, and the little pit-houses used for textile work and crafts.

### Food and drink

The diet rested on what the North provides. **Grain** — barley above all, oats and rye — went to bread (often flatbread), porridge, and ale. **Dairy** was central: milk, butter (a store of wealth, used to pay rent), cheese, and *skyr*-like soured products that stored well. **Fish** — cod, herring, salmon — was dried, salted, or fermented; stockfish from northern Norway was already a trade good. **Meat** came from cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, and horses (horseflesh carried ritual associations and was banned as a Christianising measure in Iceland), supplemented by hunting, fowling, and, across the north, seal and whale. Ale was the everyday drink and the social lubricant of every feast; **mead** — honey-based, and honey was scarce and often imported — was the prestige drink of poetry and myth, praised out of proportion to how often most people can have tasted it.

### Clothing, and the helmet that never was

Textile finds, above all from graves and from the waterlogged layers of Hedeby and other towns, let us reconstruct dress in unusual detail. The foundation was **wool** — homespun, woven on warp-weighted looms, dyed with madder, woad, and local plants — with **linen** (flax) for undertunics where flax grew, and **silk** as an imported luxury, found in elite graves at Birka and elsewhere, arriving via the eastern trade routes. Men wore tunics, trousers, cloaks fastened with a brooch, and leg-wraps; women of status wore the distinctive **apron-dress** (*hangerock*): a tubular woollen dress held by straps fastened at the front with paired oval **brooches** — the single most recognisable item of Viking Age female dress — with strings of glass beads and small tools sometimes hung between them. Fur, leather, and walrus-hide rope complete a wardrobe built for cold seas.

> **The horned helmet misconception.** No horned helmet has ever been found from the Viking Age, and none is depicted in Viking Age art. Horned headgear belongs to the European Bronze Age (the Veksø helmets from Denmark are roughly two thousand years older) and to the imagination of the nineteenth century, when costume designers for Wagner's operas put horns on Norse heroes and the image stuck forever. Real Viking Age helmets — see Section 7 — were simple iron or leather headpieces, and rare. The horns are theatre, not archaeology.

### Hygiene: better than their reputation

The archaeological toolkit of daily grooming is everywhere in Viking Age graves: **combs** (of antler and bone, among the commonest finds of all), tweezers, razors, nail-cleaners, and **ear spoons**. The English chronicler John of Wallingford, writing in the thirteenth century, complained that Danish men in England combed their hair daily, bathed on Saturdays, and changed their clothes regularly — and thereby seduced English wives, which tells us more about the chronicler than the Danes, but aligns with the finds. The Old Norse word for Saturday, *laugardagr*, literally means "washing day."

The most quoted hygiene text is the Arab traveller **Ibn Fadlan's** account (922 CE) of the Rus' on the Volga, who washed each morning from a shared basin passed hand to hand — a passage usually quoted to make them sound filthy, though it actually describes a daily washing ritual performed even in freezing weather. Ibn Fadlan was a fastidious outsider describing customs not his own, with a traveller's eye for the shocking; his account is invaluable precisely because it is an eyewitness source, but it is *his* account of one group of Rus' traders, not an ethnography of Scandinavia, and it should be quoted as such.

### Health and medicine

Honesty requires admitting how little we know. There was no medical profession in the modern sense. Healing was household work, largely women's work, mixing competent practical care — wound management, bone-setting, herbal remedies from plants like plantain and yarrow, whose uses persist in later Scandinavian herbals — with charms, amulets, and appeals to the gods and land spirits. The sagas remember healing as a gift, sometimes a miraculous one. The skeletal record shows what medicine faced: osteoarthritis everywhere, dental wear and abscesses, healed fractures, tuberculosis, and occasional trepanation-like intervention. Life expectancy at birth was low — probably in the twenties or thirties, dragged down by child mortality — but those who survived childhood commonly lived into their fifties and sixties, and grave populations include weathered elders.

## 5. Women and Family

### Standing before the law

Viking Age women were subordinate to men in law and public life — they did not (with rare exceptions) speak at the þing, hold the goðorð, or lead war bands — but they were legal persons with enforceable rights, more so than many of their European contemporaries. A woman could own property, inherit land (daughters inherited, though typically after sons in the lines that law codes specify), receive back her dowry and her morning-gift on divorce, and — as saga after saga dramatises — **initiate divorce** herself, by declaring it before witnesses for stated grounds. Widows of standing ran estates; the great runestone at Hillersjö in Sweden records a woman named Gerlög inheriting, through a chain of deaths, from husband, son, and daughter, and raising the stone to say so. Women commissioned runestones in substantial numbers — a public, expensive, permanent form of self-assertion in stone.

### The household and the keys

The centre of female power was the household economy. Farms ran on textiles: cloth was not a by-product but a currency — in Iceland, standardised homespun (*vaðmál*) functioned as legal tender, its value set by law. The loom, the store-rooms, the dairy, the brewing, and the management of food stocks were the mistress of the house's domain, and the great iron **keys** found in women's graves — worn at the belt, sometimes finely made — are read by archaeologists as the emblem of that office: she locked the chests, the stores, the house. Women appear in the record as poets (*skáldkonur* are few but attested), as traders (the wealthy female graves at Kaupang and elsewhere include scales and weights), and as settlers: *Landnámabók* names women among Iceland's primary settlers, and Aud the Deep-Minded, who led her household to Iceland after her husband's and son's deaths and freed her slaves on land she gave them, is remembered as one of the settlement's great figures.

### Marriage, fosterage, children

Marriage was an alliance between families, negotiated through the betrothal contract and the transfer of property — her *heimanfylgja* (dowry) and his *mundr* (bride-price) — and normally required, in law, the consent of her guardian; whether the bride's own consent was genuinely required is debated, whatever later law codes say. **Fosterage** bound families beyond blood: children were raised in other households, creating bonds the Icelanders considered stronger than kinship, and out-fostering a child to a social inferior was a calculated act of alliance or humility. Children were named — often for dead ancestors, whose luck was thought to travel with the name — and were working members of the farm from an early age. The sources honestly attest a darker practice: the **exposure of infants** (*útburðr*), abandoning unwanted newborns, typically those deformed or born to families unable to feed them. It was lawful in the Pagan order; its prohibition was one of the conditions Iceland's Christians demanded at the conversion, and the fact that the old practice was briefly tolerated even then, under the settlement around the year 1000, shows how deep it ran.

### Bj 581 and the shield-maiden question

The richest test case for women and war is grave **Bj 581** at Birka: a tenth-century chamber grave containing a sword, axe, spear, armour-piercing arrows, two shields, a battle-knife, gaming pieces, and two horses — the full equipment of a high-status warrior, buried in the garrison town's military quarter. The bones were long assumed male on the strength of the goods alone. In **2017, ancient DNA analysis showed the buried individual was female**. That finding is solid. What it proves is contested: the publishing team reads the burial as a female professional warrior and commander (the gaming pieces suggest tactical leadership); critics have questioned whether all the bones in the old excavation belonged to a single burial, and more broadly whether a weapon burial — for a man or a woman — proves the deceased personally fought, since weapons can mark rank, family, or office rather than biography. The DNA result stands; the interpretation remains a live scholarly debate, and this guide reports it as exactly that.

Beyond Bj 581 lie the **shield-maidens** (*skjaldmeyjar*) of saga and myth — Hervör taking up her father's sword, the valkyries choosing the slain. Saxo Grammaticus, writing around 1200, claims women once fought in Danish armies, but he is a Christian author writing centuries later with classical models in mind. Historian Judith Jesch's verdict remains the careful one: there is no good evidence that organised groups of female warriors existed, while individual women may occasionally have fought, and women unquestionably accompanied armies — and were among their victims. The shield-maiden is best read as a powerful literary and mythic figure that tells us what Norse culture could imagine, and possibly fear, about women who stepped outside the household order.

## 6. Ships and Seafaring

Everything in this guide floats on one technology: the **clinker-built ship**. Overlapping strakes of oak (or pine) riveted together over a single great keel and flexible frames produced a hull that was light, shallow-draught, and elastic — it flexed with the sea instead of fighting it, could be beached on any shore without a harbour, and could be rowed up rivers too shallow for other shipping. A square sail on a single mast gave speed before the wind (reconstructions have exceeded 11 knots), and the steering board — the *stjórnborði*, hung on the right side, whence "starboard" — steered it.

Two families of vessel matter most. The **longship** — long, narrow, many-oared, of which the 24-metre **Gokstad ship** (buried c. 890, found 1880) is the classic survivor, with 32 pairs of oars and room for some seventy men — was the warship and prestige vessel. The **knarr** was its indispensable opposite: a broad, deep, sail-dependent cargo carrier with a small crew, the ship that actually colonised the Atlantic; the Skuldelev 1 wreck from Roskilde fjord is its type specimen. The **Oseberg ship** (buried 834), found in 1904 beneath its mound in Norway with carved spiral prow and the richest grave goods of the age, shows the type raised to aristocratic art.

Navigation crossed open ocean without compass or chart, by sun, stars, wind, swell, birds, and whale-roads remembered as sailing directions ("from Norway sail west, keeping Iceland's mountains in sight in clear weather…"). The **sunstone** hypothesis — that Norse sailors used a birefringent crystal, Icelandic spar, to locate the sun in overcast skies — rests on one saga reference and experimental demonstrations that the method works in principle; crystals have been found in maritime contexts but none in an unambiguous Viking Age navigational setting. It is a legitimate hypothesis, labelled as such, not an established fact.

## 7. War

### Weapons and armour

The prestige weapon was the **sword**: a pattern-welded blade in the earlier period, its core built of twisted rods of iron and steel forge-welded to produce shimmering watered patterns, later giving way to superior monosteel. The most famous blades bear the inlaid mark **+VLFBERH+T** — some 170 Ulfberht swords survive across Europe, made (probably in the Frankish Rhineland) over three centuries; metallurgical analysis shows some were forged from crucible steel of remarkable purity, imported perhaps from Central Asia, while others are competent imitations. The **axe** — from the small hand axe to the broad-bladed Dane axe of the eleventh century — was the common weapon of the Scandinavian fighting man, with the **spear** (both thrown and thrusting, the cheapest and commonest weapon of all), the single-edged **seax**, and the **bow** (a serious weapon at sea and in sieges; a complete yew longbow survives from Hedeby).

Against this offence stood surprisingly little defence. A wooden shield, often with an iron boss, was universal in battle. **Mail** was ruinously expensive and correspondingly rare — a single shirt represented years of labour. **Helmets** were rarer still: exactly one complete Viking Age helmet, the **Gjermundbu** helmet from Norway (tenth century), has been found — a practical iron cap with a spectacle-like face guard, and nothing like the horned fantasy. Most warriors fought in their woollen clothes. The **shield wall** of overlapping shields described in the poems is real enough in the sources, though modern scholars debate how formal a tactic it was, and much Viking fighting was never pitched battle at all: it was the raid, the ambush, the burned farm, the seized bridge.

### Berserkir and úlfhéðnar

The **berserkir** — warriors who fought in a trance-like fury, biting their shields and howling in *Ynglinga saga*, impervious (the sagas claim) to fire and iron — and their rarer wolf-clad counterparts, the **úlfhéðnar**, belong to the court retinues of kings in the literary sources and to Odin's own war-band in myth. Snorri describes them as Odin's men who "went without mail and were wild as dogs or wolves." What stands behind the literature is disputed: proposals range from ritual warrior sodalities with animal identities, through battlefield shock tactics and self-induced frenzy, to the famous suggestion that they drugged themselves with fly agaric mushrooms — a theory popularised in 1784 on no documentary evidence whatsoever, and now largely discounted by historians (see the table in Section 13). Treat the berserk as a genuine feature of Norse martial imagination whose historical kernel cannot be recovered with confidence.

### The economics of raiding, and armies

Raiding was a business. Monasteries were targeted not from special hatred of Christianity — though Pagans surely enjoyed the irony — but because they were undefended storehouses of portable wealth: silver altar vessels, jewelled book covers, and people, who could be ransomed or sold. The profits were displayed, gifted to followers, buried in hoards, and converted into land and marriages at home; a successful raiding season was a young man's capital. But by the 860s the scale changed: the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*'s **Great Heathen Army** (*micel here*) of 865 stayed in England for years, conquered kingdoms, and settled — raiding maturing into invasion and colonisation. The age also fortified: the geometric **Trelleborg-type ring fortresses** of Denmark (Trelleborg, Fyrkat, Aggersborg, Nonnebakken), dendro-dated to around 980 in Harald Bluetooth's reign, are royal power stamped on the landscape in perfect circles; and the **Danevirke**, the great earthwork system across the base of Jutland — begun before the Viking Age and repeatedly rebuilt during it — defended Denmark's southern door against the Franks and Saxons.

## 8. Trade and Towns

Raiding's quieter twin built the age's most durable monuments: its towns. Scandinavia entered the Viking Age with no true towns and left it with several.

**Ribe** in Jutland, founded around 705–710, is the oldest — a planned trading place laid out in plots along a street, where Frisian and Scandinavian merchants already met before the first recorded raid. **Hedeby**, at the base of Jutland where a short portage linked Baltic to North Sea, grew into the North's greatest emporium: at its tenth-century height perhaps 1,500–2,000 inhabitants behind a semicircular rampart, with its own mint, churches, and streets of craftspeople — the Arab traveller al-Tartushi, who visited in the 960s, left a bemused account of its singing, its diet, and its siege of his sensibilities. **Birka**, on an island in Lake Mälaren (Sweden), flourished from about 750 to 975: some 700–1,000 people, a garrisoned hillfort, chamber graves rich with Byzantine silk and Islamic silver, and a famous visitor — the Frankish missionary Ansgar, whose *Life* (written by Rimbert) is our best contemporary text on a Norse town. **Kaupang** in Vestfold, Norway — the name simply means "market place" — served the Skagerrak trade from about 800 into the mid-tenth century. These were seasonal worlds at first, wintering-over only gradually, connected to a web of smaller markets, beach-trading sites, and magnate farms.

The economy ran on **weighed silver**, not coin. A trader's essential kit was a balance and a set of weights — cubo-octahedral and spherical weights appear in graves and hoards across the Norse world — and the currency was **hacksilver**: coins, ingots, and jewellery cut into pieces and valued by weight and purity, tested by nicking and bending. Local coinage came slowly and stayed politically charged: a few ninth-century issues at Hedeby and Ribe, then the substantial royal coinages of the late tenth and eleventh centuries as kings like Olaf Tryggvason, Cnut, and Olaf Haraldsson used minted silver as an instrument and advertisement of kingship. Amber, furs, down, walrus ivory, slaves, and iron went out; silver, silk, wine, glass, salt, and quernstones of Rhenish lava came in.

Craft production filled the towns: smiths forging tools and pattern-welded blades, bronze-casters filling carved moulds with oval brooches by the thousand, glass-bead furnaces remelting imported glass into necklaces, comb-makers working reindeer antler — and amber workers polishing the North's own gold. The quality flowered in the **art styles**, which succeed one another so reliably that archaeologists use them to date graves and hoards:

- **Broa / Oseberg style** (c. 775–850): elegant, ribbon-like gripping beasts, at their most luxurious in the carved wood of the Oseberg burial.
- **Borre style** (c. 850–950): tight, triangular-headed ring-chain interlace, named for the ship burial at Borre in Norway; the look of a thousand cast brooches.
- **Jellinge style** (c. 870–985): sinuous, S-shaped ribbon animals, named for the royal monuments at Jelling in Denmark.
- **Mammen style** (c. 960–1025): lush, fleshy beasts and birds entwined in acanthus-like tendrils, named for a magnificently embroidered and axe-rich grave at Mammen.
- **Ringerike style** (c. 1000–1075): slender, spiralling tendrils and striding lions, named for the carved stones of the Ringerike district in Norway.
- **Urnes style** (c. 1050–1150): the last and most elegant — slim, looping beasts biting their own bodies in figure-of-eight knots, named for the carvings of Urnes stave church, and carried into the Christian Middle Ages.

## 9. Language and Literacy

The common tongue of all these peoples was **Old Norse**, a North Germanic language whose speakers from Greenland to Kyiv could understand one another (they called it simply *dǫnsk tunga*, "the Danish tongue"). Its descendants are Icelandic, Norwegian, Swedish, Danish, and Faroese; Icelandic has changed so conservatively that Icelanders still read the sagas with effort rather than translation.

The writing system of the age was the **Younger Futhark**: just **sixteen runes**, reduced from the twenty-four of the older Elder Futhark at the very threshold of the Viking Age. Why a language *gaining* sounds (as Old Norse vowels diversified) would *shed* letters is a standing puzzle; explanations range from a deliberate, rapid reform spread through the trading networks, to the runes' primary use as a practical labelling system where ambiguity was tolerable — one rune stood for several sounds, and context did the rest. What is certain is the result: the Viking Age runic corpus is large, practical, and overwhelmingly carved on stone.

The **runestone** was Scandinavia's public monument — some three thousand survive, nearly all in Sweden, the great majority raised in the late tenth and eleventh centuries by Christian families. They are memorials, inheritance claims, and boasts in one: "X raised this stone in memory of Y…" The grandest are the **Jelling stones** of Denmark. The smaller was raised by Gorm the Old for his wife Thyra; the larger, by their son **Harald Bluetooth**, declares in carved runes around an image of Christ that Harald "won for himself all Denmark and Norway and made the Danes Christian" — a king's conversion, announced as a conquest. Compare that monumental voice with the **everyday runes** later scratched on wooden sticks in Bergen's Bryggen wharf — merchant tags, love notes ("Ást min heitir…" — love-sick graffiti eight centuries old), alphabet exercises, obscenities — and you see runes as the age's everything-script: prayer, title-deed, and text message. (The Bergen inscriptions are twelfth- to fourteenth-century, a generation or three after the Viking Age proper; they show the tradition's long everyday afterlife, and are labelled medieval here for honesty's sake.)

Alongside the carved letters ran the oral world. Memory was a profession: the **skalds**, court poets who composed in the fiendishly intricate *drótkvætt* metre — interlaced word order, heiti (poetic synonyms), and kennings ("whale-road" for sea, "sword-storm" for battle) — produced praise poems that were the age's news service, archive, and propaganda. Their verses, embedded centuries later in prose sagas, are among our earliest Norse sources precisely because the metre locked the wording in place. And then came the great preservation accident: **Iceland**, Christianised, Latin-literate, sheep-rich (vellum is calf and sheep skin) and saga-haunted, spent the twelfth to fourteenth centuries writing down — in the vernacular, on an unmatched scale for so small a nation — the poems, myths, histories, and family sagas that preserve the Viking Age's memory of itself. Nearly everything literary in this guide survives because Iceland wrote it down. That is a debt and a distortion both: the age's voice reaches us mostly in an Icelandic accent.

## 10. Belief in Daily Life

*(The religion of the North — its gods, myths, rituals, and sacred specialists — is treated in full in a companion guide to Norse Paganism. This section covers only how belief wove through ordinary life in the Viking Age.)*

For most people most of the time, religion was not theology; it was practice at home. The farm had its cult: offerings to the *landvættir*, the land spirits whose goodwill the harvest needed (Icelandic law, in a clause surviving from the Christianising period, forbade approaching land with dragon-prowed ships lest the spirits be frightened — the carved prow had to come off); reverence for ancestors, whose burial mound anchored the family's title to its land; and the *dísir*, female powers honoured at winter feasts. A person's **luck** (*hamingja*, *gæfa*) was felt as a real, quasi-substance — attached to families and kings, transferable, losable — and much practical religion was luck-management: choosing the day, the name, the omen before any venture.

The **conversion** to Christianity came not in a generation but over some two and a half centuries, at different speeds in different fjords: Ansgar's fragile missions in the ninth century; Harald Bluetooth's Denmark in the 960s; Norway's conversion driven hard by Olaf Tryggvason and Olaf Haraldsson around 995–1030; Sweden's long, uneven road into the twelfth century; and **Iceland's famous legal conversion around the year 1000**, when the law-speaker Thorgeir Thorkelsson, after a day and a night lying silent under a cloak, ruled that the community would have one law and one faith — with Pagan sacrifice, infant exposure, and horse-eating temporarily permitted in private, concessions soon withdrawn. The archaeology shows the untidiness the texts tidy away: graves holding a cross and a Thor's hammer side by side, hammer-pendants and cross-pendants cast from the same moulds, Christian burials in old family mounds. People hedged. A traveller in *Landnámabók* is said to have believed in Christ but sacrificed to Thor for seafaring — not hypocrisy but portfolio religion, from an age when a god was somebody you had a relationship with, not a proposition you assented to. The world of this guide — the one where a Heathen Third Path or any modern practice looks back for roots — begins in that slow, negotiated transition, not in a single day of decision.

## 11. Death

Death is the Viking Age's great informant: because grave goods were buried and largely left alone, the dead have told us more than the living ever wrote down. But burial was never one rite. It varied by region, period, wealth, and family custom to a degree that forbids any single "Viking funeral."

**Cremation** dominated in much of Sweden and parts of Norway: the body burned on a pyre with goods and animals, the ashes and burnt fragments gathered into a mound or a stone setting shaped like a ship. Inhumation dominated in Denmark and in the colonies. The rich were buried in **chambers** — timber rooms built underground and furnished like a compressed household (Birka's chamber graves are the type case) — or, at the summit of society, in **ships**. The Oseberg burial (834) interred a ship of nearly 24 metres beneath a clay-sealed mound, containing the bodies of two women (one elderly, one younger, whose identities — queen Åsa, mother of Halfdan the Black, has been proposed for the elder — remain debated), a carved wagon, sledges, beds, tapestries, and the sacrificed bodies of horses, dogs, and oxen. Gokstad (c. 890) gave a man of about forty a ship, small boats, horses, and a peacock. Animal sacrifice at funerals is widely attested in graves; human sacrifice at funerals rests chiefly on the textual testimony — Ibn Fadlan's account of a Rus funeral on the Volga, with its ritual killing of a slave woman, and Adam of Bremen's description of the Uppsala temple — and on a small number of ambiguous graves (double burials where one body shows violence). That evidence deserves to be reported, not inflated.

The mounds themselves were statements: placed on skylines and along sailing routes, they marked ancestry and ownership for everyone who passed. Grave goods, read carefully, tell us about craft, trade networks, dress, and belief — a balance and weights speak of the eastern trade, a silk cap of Byzantium, a Buddha figurine found at Helgö in Sweden (cast in the Swat Valley, carried the whole breadth of Asia) of how far the world's goods could travel. What the goods cannot tell us is what the mourners believed would happen next; the afterlife in the sources — Valhalla for the battle-slain chosen by Odin, Hel's grey realm, the goddess Rán's net for the drowned, the mound-dwelling dead who were still neighbours — is a patchwork, regional and contradictory, and Christians tidied it just as Pagans had. We see the furniture of death far more clearly than its theology.

## 12. The End of the Age

The Viking Age did not end in defeat; it dissolved into success. Between about 950 and 1150, the Scandinavian world transformed itself:

**States formed.** Loose chiefdoms consolidated into the three kingdoms. Kings built towns, mints, and fortresses (the Trelleborg ring forts, Jelling's royal complex), taxed trade, and issued laws in their own names. The freelance political space in which a chieftain could equip a fleet and seek his fortune — the precondition of the Viking phenomenon — steadily closed.

**Christianity organised.** The Church brought literacy, administration, stone architecture, and an international network no Pagan king could match. By 1100 the North had bishops; by 1152/53 an archbishopric at Nidaros in Norway. Christian kingship and the old honour culture negotiated a long settlement — feud became crime, the Church attacked slavery of fellow Christians, and the law codes were written down under royal authority.

**The frontier closed.** The targets of the early raids had fortified and organised (Alfred's burhs, the Loire and Seine defences, fortified bridges under Charles the Bald's edict of 864); England and Francia were no longer soft. The flow of Islamic silver east of the Baltic thinned around the turn of the millennium as the dirham mines and routes faltered, and western silver replaced it. Meanwhile the Norse diaspora assimilated: Normans spoke French, Danelaw Danes became English, the Rus' became Slavic — the diaspora's success was its own disappearance.

**And the Norse Atlantic contracted slowly.** The Western Settlement in Greenland was deserted by about 1350 and the Eastern Settlement faded in the fifteenth century — victims of climate cooling (the Little Ice Age's onset), the collapse of the walrus-ivory market, and isolation when Norwegian shipping withered. That is a *medieval* story, two to four centuries after the Viking Age, and it should never be backdated into it: the Greenland Norse were not "the last Vikings"; they were medieval Catholic farmers who happened to live far away.

What ended, then, around 1050–1100 was not a people but a mode of life: independent, sea-borne, kin-organised, Pagan, and expansionist. The Scandinavians who remained were medieval Europeans — but Europeans with runestones on the hills, sagas in the farmhouses, and a seafaring genius that would carry their descendants, in time, across every ocean on earth.

## 13. Sources and Evidence: How We Know

Every claim in this guide stands on one or more of five kinds of evidence, each with its own strength and its own trap.

**Archaeology** is the backbone: graves, hoards, towns, ships, fortresses, and the daily rubbish of a thousand farms. It speaks of what people did rather than what they said, and modern methods — dendrochronology dating timbers to the year, isotope analysis tracing where a buried person grew up, textile and residue analysis — keep extracting new testimony from old ground. Its trap is survival bias: organic life rots, iron rusts, and the rich bury more noticeably than the poor.

**Runology** gives the age its own voice: inscriptions made by Scandinavians for Scandinavians, contemporary with the events. Names, memorials, claims, boasts — terse, public, and undyingly biased toward the prosperous families who could afford stone.

**Texts** divide into two very different kinds. *Contemporary outsiders* — the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*, Frankish annals, Alcuin, Adam of Bremen, and Muslim geographers and travellers like Ibn Fadlan and Ibn Khordadbeh — report what they saw or heard, through the eyes of victims, missionaries, and merchants. *Norse literature itself* — eddic and skaldic poetry, the sagas, *Landnámabók*, the law codes — was overwhelmingly written down in the twelfth to fourteenth centuries, two hundred years and a religious revolution after the Viking Age ended. The sagas preserve genuine memory, genuine poetry older than the prose around it, and a great deal of thirteenth-century Iceland wearing tenth-century clothes. **The two-hundred-year gap is the single most important caution in Viking studies**: a saga is evidence for what later Icelanders believed about their ancestors, and only partially, carefully, for the ancestors themselves.

**Place names** are fossils of settlement: every Danelaw *-by*, every Icelandic *-staðir* and *-vík*, maps where Norse speakers actually lived and how densely, long after their bones are gone.

**Genetics** is the newest witness. Large recent ancient-DNA studies (notably the 2020 population-genomics survey of over 400 Viking Age genomes) have confirmed the broad pattern of the sources — gene flow from Scandinavia into the diaspora, Norwegians toward the Atlantic islands, Danes toward England, Swedes eastward; settlers of mixed Norse and local ancestry in Iceland and elsewhere; and individuals like Bj 581 whose sex the DNA settled. Genetics answers questions of movement and kinship superbly; it cannot tell us what anyone believed, spoke, or called themselves, and honest scholars state its findings with those limits attached.

### Myths vs evidence

| Popular claim | The evidence | Honest verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Vikings wore horned helmets | No Viking Age find or depiction exists; horned helmets are Bronze Age (Veksø) and operatic (19th-century Wagner costumes) | **Myth** |
| Berserks fought drugged on fly agaric mushrooms | Proposed in 1784 by Samuel Ödmann with no textual source; no saga mentions mushroom use; historians largely discount it | **Unsupported speculation** |
| Shield-maidens: organised female warriors | Shield-maidens are literary/mythic figures; Saxo's claims are late and modelled; Bj 581 proves one high-status woman was buried with full warrior equipment, and aDNA (2017) confirmed her sex | **Largely literary**; individual cases debated on real evidence |
| The *blóðörn* ("blood eagle") ritual of torture-execution | Described in a handful of skaldic verses and later sagas about Ivar the Boneless and others; scholars debate whether the verses describe a ritual at all or were literal-minded misreadings of poetic imagery by later prose authors | **Unresolved debate** — possibly a literary misunderstanding; not established practice |
| "Half of Iceland's settlers were women" / "50% Norse women" | aDNA and settlement records show Iceland's founding population mixed Norse and Gaelic ancestry; mitochondrial DNA studies indicate a large share of female settlers were of Gaelic (Irish/Scottish) origin, while male lines were predominantly Norse — but precise population fractions cannot be recovered and "50%" figures vary with method | **Oversimplified** — the real picture is mixed ancestry with a sex bias, not a clean percentage |
| Vikings were filthy barbarians | Combs, ear spoons, razors, and washing-day customs abound in the record; John of Wallingford complained Danes bathed weekly | **False** — by the standards of their century, notably groomed |
| Vikings discovered America | Norse settlement c. 1000–1021 confirmed archaeologically at L'Anse aux Meadows, five centuries before Columbus — on land long inhabited by Indigenous peoples, whom the sagas call *skrælingjar* | **True, carefully worded**: first *European* presence; the continent was never "undiscovered" to its own peoples |

## 14. Further Reading

All of the following are real, serious works, ordered roughly from the most welcoming to the most specialised.

- **Neil Price, *The Children of Ash and Elm: A History of the Vikings* (2020).** The best single modern account: deep, strange, humane archaeology-driven history by a leading scholar of the period. If you read one book, read this one.
- **Anders Winroth, *The Age of the Vikings* (2014).** A lucid, economical survey of the age's politics, economy, and emigration, especially strong on why the Viking phenomenon happened when it did.
- **Stefan Brink & Neil Price (eds.), *The Viking World* (2008).** The standard scholarly reference: dozens of short chapters by specialists on every topic in this guide, from ship technology to slavery to the Rus'.
- **Else Roesdahl, *The Vikings* (3rd ed., 2016).** A classic, clear, archaeologist's overview, repeatedly updated — for decades the standard introduction in English.
- **Judith Jesch, *Women in the Viking Age* (1991).** The foundational study of women's lives in the period, careful and unsentimental; still the benchmark. Her ***The Viking Diaspora* (2015)** treats the whole Norse expansion as one interconnected world of movement and settlement.
- **James Graham-Campbell, *The Viking World* (4th ed., 2013)** and his illustrated surveys of Viking art and artefacts — the visual record, expertly curated.
- **Primary sources in translation:** the *Poetic Edda* (Carolyne Larrington's translation is the standard), *The Saga of the Icelanders* material in the Penguin classics of the family sagas, Ibn Fadlan's account in *Ibn Fadlan and the Land of Darkness* (trans. Paul Lunde & Caroline Stone), and Rimbert's *Life of Ansgar* for Birka and the missions.

## Closing Word

Strip away the horns, the helmets, and the Hollywood growl, and the Viking Age becomes more interesting, not less: a small farming population at the cold edge of Europe that built the best ships in the world, traded with Baghdad, argued law on an Icelandic lava field, buried their grandmothers under ships, and changed the map from Newfoundland to Kyiv. The record they left — in graves, stones, and stories — is fragmentary, contradictory, and endlessly alive to careful reading. That reading, evidence first and romance second, is what this guide has tried to practise on every page.

*— Yrsa Freydisdottir*
