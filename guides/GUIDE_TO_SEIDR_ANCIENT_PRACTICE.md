# Seiðr — A Guide to the Authentic Practices of the Ancient Norse

*By Yrsa Freydisdottir*

*A guide to what is actually known — from the medieval texts and the ground itself — about the magic the Old Norse called seiðr. Written for readers who would rather have the honest fragments than a beautiful invention.*

---

## Before Anything Else: How Honest This Guide Has to Be

Seiðr is the most evocative word in Old Norse religion, and the worst documented. Everything in this guide stands under three hard facts, and any reader who keeps them in mind will understand the subject better than most books on it manage.

**First, the sources are late and they are Christian.** The sagas and poems that describe seiðr were written down in the twelfth, thirteenth, and fourteenth centuries — two hundred years and more after Iceland and Norway converted to Christianity. Their authors were learned Christian men describing a pagan past they had never lived. Some of them, like Snorri Sturluson, were antiquarians who genuinely valued the old lore; others wrote seiðr as villainy, comedy, or both at once. None of them wrote as practitioners teaching a craft.

**Second, no seiðr manual survives.** There is no grimoire, no ritual script, no initiatory curriculum, no first-person account by a völva of what she did or what she experienced. What survives is a scatter: a paragraph here, a scene there, a grave under a Danish fortress, a clause in a law code forbidding the whole thing. This guide is built from that scatter, and it does not pretend the scatter is a system.

**Third, anyone who claims a complete, unbroken, surviving seiðr tradition is inventing it.** Modern seiðr exists — people practise it, seriously and sincerely — but it is reconstruction from these same fragments, not survival. That distinction runs through everything below.

To keep the distinction visible, every major claim in this guide carries one of four labels:

- **ATTESTED** — stated in a named medieval source. Attested does not mean *true*; it means the source says it.
- **ARCHAEOLOGY** — supported by physical finds.
- **INFERENCE** — scholarly reasoning from evidence; plausible, argued, not proven.
- **RECONSTRUCTION** — modern practice or guesswork. Legitimate as modern practice; never ancient fact.

Where a claim has no label, it is background — chronology, definitions, the kind of thing no scholar disputes. Where it matters, the label is there.

## 1. What Seiðr Was

### The definition, as far as the sources allow one

**ATTESTED.** In the Old Norse sources, *seiðr* is a form of magic concerned above all with **knowledge of the future and influence over fate** — and, by extension, over minds, weather, health, and life itself. A practitioner performs seiðr (the verb is *efla seið*, "to perform/increase seiðr"; the working itself can be called a *seiðr* as an event) typically from a raised platform, with sung or chanted accompaniment, in order to summon or compel unseen powers to answer questions or to act in the world. The characteristic products of seiðr in the texts are prophecy, weather-working, and harm sent at a distance — sickness, madness, blighted luck, death.

It is worth saying what seiðr is not, in the sources' own usage. It is not a general word for all magic. Old Norse has a rich magical vocabulary, and the texts distinguish its terms more carefully than modern retellings usually do.

### Seiðr and its neighbours

**ATTESTED** (usage across the saga and poetic corpus):

- **Spá** — prophecy itself: the art and product of foreseeing. A *spákona* is a prophetess; *spá* names the divinatory act, where *seiðr* names one powerful technology for achieving it. Not every prophecy in the sagas involves seiðr, and not every seiðr is a prophecy — the two overlap heavily but are not synonyms.
- **Galdr** — spellcraft worked through the voice: chanted or sung charms, from *gala*, "to crow, to sing." Galdr is defined by its medium — formalised, sung utterance, often in the special metre *galdralag* — rather than by a trance or a platform. Odin is the great galdr-master of the poetry. Seiðr also uses song (see the *varðlokkur* below), but the song in seiðr is an engine for summoning powers to the platform; in galdr, the song *is* the spell.
- **Runemagic** — the carving and use of runes for effect: healing, harming, love, victory. Egill Skalla-Grímsson is the sagas' great rune-worker. Rune-work in the sources is a craft of signs, learned and technical, usually solitary and silent — a different register from seiðr's communal performance.
- **Fjölkyngi** — "much-knowing": a broad learned term for great magical knowledge in general, the scholar-magician's competence. It is the umbrella under which seiðr, galdr, and other arts can all shelter.
- **Trolldómr, galdrakunnátta, forneskja** ("old-knowledge") — general or pejorative terms for sorcery, common in Christian-era writing.

The clean distinctions are the sources' own. Later centuries blurred them; the Viking Age texts, mostly, did not.

### The word itself

**INFERENCE.** The etymology of *seiðr* is genuinely debated, and the debate matters because each proposed root paints a different picture of the practice. The main candidates canvassed by philologists (the discussion begins in earnest with Dag Strömbäck's foundational 1935 study *Sejd*):

- A root meaning **"to bind"** — related to words for cords, snares, and halts; seiðr as the binding of fate, of spirits, or of a victim's mind. This reading fits the sources' emphasis on seiðr compelling unseen powers and fastening effects on distant people.
- A root shared with words for **"song" or "chant"** — seiðr as "the sung thing," fitting the centrality of the *varðlokkur*.
- Connections proposed to **"seething"** or boiling — seiðr as a heated, ecstatic state.

No candidate commands the field. The honest summary: the word was already old and its roots already obscure when the sagas were written, and any modern book that gives one etymology as settled fact is overselling. Cognate forms appear across the North Germanic world, and the practice-word travelled with the culture; the *thing*, whatever its name's origin, was recognisably one practice-complex from Greenland to the Baltic in the sources' imagination.

The people have their own vocabulary. The woman who performs seiðr is most often a **völva** (treated fully in section 3) or a **seiðkona**, "seiðr-woman"; a man is a **seiðmaðr**, "seiðr-man" — a word the sources rarely use kindly. The prophetess as such is a **spákona**. These titles overlap in use but are not identical, and the völva is the figure the whole complex turns on.

## 2. The Primary Sources, One by One

Everything known about seiðr descends from a small shelf of texts. Here they are, in roughly the order a student should meet them, with what each actually attests — and what it does not.

### Ynglinga saga, chapter 7 — Snorri's summary

**ATTESTED.** Snorri Sturluson's *Ynglinga saga* (part of *Heimskringla*, c. 1230) contains the single most-quoted passage on seiðr in all of Old Norse literature. Snorri says that **Freyja — a Vanir goddess and a priestess (*blótgyðja*) — first taught seiðr to the Æsir**, it being customary among the Vanir. He says Odin learned and practised it, that by seiðr one could learn the fates of men and things not yet happened, and also bring on death, ill-luck, or sickness, and take wit or strength from one person and give it to another. And he says the crucial social fact: **this magic was attended by such great *ergi* — unmanliness, sexual and social shame — that it was thought shameful for men to practise it, and so the art was taught to the priestesses.**

Hold the whole passage at arm's length even as you use it. Snorri wrote two centuries after conversion, rationalising mythology into a history of human kings and cults (his Odin is a migrating chieftain-sorcerer, not straightforwardly a god). What chapter 7 attests beyond argument is what a learned thirteenth-century Icelander believed the tradition said: seiðr comes from the Vanir and from Freyja; it is powerful, fate-working, and female-coded, with shame attaching to the man who takes it up. Whether Viking Age people would have phrased it so is a further question — **INFERENCE** — but the gender coding Snorri describes is corroborated all over the independent sources below, which is why this chapter anchors every serious study.

### Eiríks saga rauða, chapter 4 — the fullest account we have

**ATTESTED.** The saga of Erik the Red (preserved in two redactions, Hauksbók and Skálholtsbók) contains the most detailed surviving description of a seiðr performance, set in Norse Greenland around the year 1000, in a time of famine. The community sends for **Þorbjörg**, called *lítilvölva*, "the little völva," a prophetess travelling between farms. Her sister-prophetesses are dead of sickness; she is the last of nine. What the saga gives us, item by item, is the closest thing to a seiðr liturgy that exists:

- **Her welcome.** She is received with honour; a high seat is prepared for her. The head of household asks after her comfort with formal courtesy, and she answers that the matter depends on how she is treated. Hospitality is part of the technology — she works when she is feasted.
- **Her costume.** She wears a blue (or black-blue; the word is *blár*) cloak of lambskin set with stones down to the hem; glass beads around her neck; a hood of black lambskin lined with white cat-skin; **gloves of cat's skin, white and furry within**; shoes of calfskin with long thongs and big brass knobs at the ends; and a staff in her hand — knobbed, inlaid with brass, set with stones about the knob. At her belt hangs a great pouch of leather holding her charms (*taufr*), which, the saga says, she needed for her knowledge.
- **Her seat.** The **seiðhjallr** — the seiðr platform or high seat — is made ready for her, and on it a cushion stuffed with hen feathers.
- **Her meal.** Before working she is fed a special meal: a porridge of goat's milk and the **hearts of every kind of animal available** there.
- **The song.** Seiðr cannot proceed without the **varðlokkur** — "ward-songs" or "guardian-summoning songs" — which must be sung by someone who knows them. In the settlement only one person does: **Guðríðr Þorbjarnardóttir**, a Christian woman, taught the songs by her foster-mother in Iceland. Guðríðr hesitates — she is a Christian, and this is heathen work — and is persuaded by honour, obligation, and Þorbjörg's own dignity ("I do not think you the worse woman for it"). She takes her place in the **circle of women** around the platform and sings the varðlokkur so beautifully that the saga says no one present thought they had heard the song sung with so lovely a voice.
- **The working.** Þorbjörg reports that the spirits (*náttúrur*, "natures/powers") who had before refused to obey and kept their distance have now been drawn near; many things hidden from her are now clear. She prophesies: the famine will lift as spring comes; the sickness will pass; and she gives Guðríðr a personal prophecy of her marriages and her descendants — a famous passage, since Guðríðr's line leads to bishops of Iceland.
- **Her payment.** She is rewarded with gifts, as a specialist of standing is.

Everything in this scene deserves its label respected. It is **ATTESTED** as a thirteenth/fourteenth-century saga description — extraordinarily specific, internally coherent, and consistent with the archaeological staff finds discussed below — and scholars since Strömbäck have treated it as resting on genuine traditional memory of how seiðr looked. It is not a transcript. No Viking Age Greenlander wrote it, and its most cinematic details cannot be independently verified. But if you want to know what a "seiðr ritual" means at all in the evidence, it means *this scene*, and everything else is commentary on it.

### Laxdæla saga — seiðr as a weapon in a family feud

**ATTESTED.** *Laxdæla saga* (c. 1240s) gives seiðr in its darker saga register. **Kotkel**, a Hebridean immigrant seiðmaðr, and his wife Gríma and their sons practise seiðr as hired malice: Kotkel mounts a platform — note the platform again, in a hostile context — and raises by seiðr a lethal storm that kills Þórðr Ingunnarson at sea. The community's response is legal and violent: the family is prosecuted for sorcery and eventually destroyed. Elsewhere in the saga, **Þuríðr**, wife of Geirmundr, uses seiðr in her own feud-working. Laxdæla attests the saga-world's working assumptions: seiðr can kill at a distance and raise weather; it is purchasable; it is performed from a raised seat even outdoors; and it sits in the law's grey zone — dangerous, dishonourable in an enemy's hands, prosecuted when it kills.

### Gísla saga Súrssonar — seiðr, the vétt, and a chill of fate

**ATTESTED.** In *Gísla saga*, seiðr threads through the doom of the hero: a seiðr is worked — associated with the sorcerer Þorgrímr nef and a sacrifice to Freyr — that lays the curse-structure on Gísli's outlawry, and the saga links the working with the *vétt*, the local land-spirit in its stone, whose favour is withdrawn from the farm. The exact mechanics are murky in the text (deliberately, one suspects; the saga wants atmosphere, not a manual), but Gísla attests the connection contemporaries drew between seiðr, sacrifice, land-spirits, and laid fate — seiðr as the art of setting hidden machinery into motion.

### Egils saga — mostly runes, and a warning about categories

**ATTESTED.** *Egils saga* is included here largely as a corrective. Egill's famous magical episodes — detecting poisoned drink with a rune and a drop of his own blood, breaking a love-spell worked by wrongly carved runes on a girl's bed ("no man shall carve runes unless he can read them well"), carving a *níðstöng* (scorn-pole) against King Eiríkr — are **runic and galdr-adjacent workings, not seiðr** in the saga's own terms. Modern popular writing files all of it under "Viking magic"; the sources file it precisely. Egill matters to this guide because he proves the Norse magical repertoire had distinct arts with distinct experts — and because the níðstöng episode (a horse's head on a pole, a formal curse spoken over it) shows how close seiðr's neighbours could come to its territory of distance-cursing without being it.

### Friðþjófs saga and Örvar-Odds saga — the völva on tour

**ATTESTED.** The legendary sagas (*fornaldarsögur*) give the völva in her itinerant professional role, drawn large. In **Örvar-Odds saga**, the völva **Heiðr** travels from farm to farm with a company of fifteen men and fifteen young women, is feasted, sits on the platform through the night, and delivers prophecies to each household member in turn — including the doom-laden prophecy of Örvar-Oddr's death by his horse Faxi, which drives the whole saga. The scene confirms Eiríks saga's picture from an independent corner of the tradition: the völva is a *touring professional*, received by rota, working at night, seated high, attended by a singing company. **Friðþjófs saga** similarly brings two seiðkonur onto a platform (*seiðhjallr* is named) to work weather-magic against the hero at sea — storm-raising from a seat, the saga's stock seiðr effect — until he drives his ship at the witches and breaks their backs. Stock scenes, late texts, legendary settings: label them ATTESTED as literature, **INFERENCE** as history — but their consistency about *how* seiðr is staged (tour, feast, platform, night, song, prophecy or storm) is a large part of why scholars think the staging was real.

### Völuspá and Baldrs draumar — questioning the dead

**ATTESTED.** The Poetic Edda preserves the völva in her most ancient-feeling form: not performing for a farm, but *being* the voice of hidden knowledge itself. In **Völuspá**, a völva — summoned and rewarded with rings and gifts by Odin ("wise in prophecy" she calls herself) — recites the whole history of the cosmos, from creation to Ragnarök and beyond, in the first person. Whether the poem's fiction is a seiðr session is debated (**INFERENCE** — many read it as the literary essence of one). In **Baldrs draumar**, there is no debate about the darker matter: Odin rides to the grave-mound east of Hel's gate and, with corpse-charming spells (*valgaldr*, "corpse-galdr"), **raises a dead völva from her grave** to question her about Baldr's dreams. She answers unwillingly — "who is this man, unknown to me, who has forced me on this troublesome journey? I was snowed on with snow, beaten with rain, drenched with dew; long was I dead." This is necromancy in the strict sense, attested in the poetry: seiðr's knowledge-seeking extended past the grave, worked by a god, with galdr, upon a dead practitioner. Note again the categories: the raising is *valgaldr*, the dead woman is a *völva*, the questioner is Odin. The arts interlock without merging.

### Hyndluljóð and Lokasenna — Freyja's arts, and the insult that defines the shame

**ATTESTED.** In **Hyndluljóð**, Freyja rides to wake the giantess Hyndla and compels a genealogy from her — a deal involving memory-beer, threats, and fire kindled around the giantess's howe until she yields. The poem is seiðr-*like* in its machinery (a female power, night, the waking of a reluctant knower) and is often read beside the seiðr material (**INFERENCE** for the classification; the poem never calls itself seiðr). In **Lokasenna** stanza 24 comes the gender-complex's proof-text: Loki, flyting Odin in Ægir's hall, taunts him — but on Samsø you practised seiðr (*síða* — the verb *síða*, "to perform seiðr"), beating a drum like the völvas do, in a wizard's shape you went among men, and that I thought the mark of an *argr* (ergi) nature. The insult only works if the hall already believes the premises: seiðr is what völvas do, drumming is part of it, and a god who does it like a woman is shamed by it. Section 5 takes up the whole complex.

### Historia Norwegiae, Saxo Grammaticus — the Latin witnesses

**ATTESTED.** The Latin-writing neighbours confirm the picture from outside the Icelandic saga tradition. The *Historia Norwegiae* (twelfth century) describes northern magicians who, with whistling and other sounds, summon and draw a spirit (*gandus* — compare the Old Norse *gandir*, seiðr's spirit-helpers) into a small figure or object, and who fall into trance-like states while their spirit travels. Saxo Grammaticus, in the *Gesta Danorum* (c. 1200), repeatedly shows sorcerers and Odin himself working shape-changing and prophetic magic, and preserves the cultural memory of seiðr-class workings in Danish legendary history. Neither Latin writer is an ethnographer, and both write through Christian contempt; but their agreement with the Icelandic material on essentials — sound as the summoning engine, trance, travelling spirit-helpers — is independent corroboration that this cluster of ideas belonged to the practice, not to one saga author's fancy.

### Ibn Fadlan — what he does *not* attest

A necessary correction. The tenth-century Arab traveller **Ibn Fadlan** describes the Rus' on the Volga, including a ship funeral, and modern writing constantly drags his account into the seiðr discussion. **He does not mention seiðr.** His account's formidable older woman, the "Angel of Death," is a funerary functionary who officiates at a killing sacrifice — grim, real-seeming, and *not* a völva performing seiðr by any evidence in the text. Citing Ibn Fadlan as a seiðr source is a category error, and it is corrected here because it is so common: the fullest eyewitness of Viking Age ritual we possess simply does not describe this practice. What we know of seiðr comes from later Scandinavian writing and from the graves. That limitation is the subject's defining condition, and this guide returns to it in section 7.

## 3. The Völva

### The word and the woman

**ATTESTED.** *Völva* derives from **völr**, a staff or wand: the völva is, in her name itself, **the staff-bearer**. The derivation is the firmest single fact in this whole guide, because it ties the literary figure to the most distinctive objects in the archaeological record (below): slender ritual staffs found in rich female graves across the Viking world. She is also called *spákona* (prophetess) and *seiðkona* (seiðr-woman); the terms are used flexibly in the texts but the völva is the titled professional — the one with the staff, the seat, the songs, and the circuit of farms.

**ATTESTED** (from the saga scenes taken together). Her social role, as the texts present it:

- **Itinerant.** She tours farms and settlements, often with a retinue (Örvar-Odds saga gives Heiðr thirty attendants), arriving by invitation in the hungry seasons.
- **Honoured.** She is feasted before she works, given the high seat, addressed with formal deference by heads of households. Þorbjörg's host asks whether she is well entertained; her prophecy is received as a public service.
- **Paid.** Gifts and rewards follow the working as a matter of course.
- **Feared.** The same sources that honour her show her shadow: to refuse a völva, mock her, or harm her is dangerous (Örvar-Oddr tries to refuse his prophecy and strikes the völva; the prophecy stands). Her knowledge makes her untouchable in a way no merely religious official of the period is.
- **Set apart.** She belongs to no household. She arrives from outside, works at night, speaks with the dead and the hidden, and leaves. Several sagas give völvas an air of great age, of a company of sisters now dead, of knowledge older than the community she serves. Whether this apartness is literary atmosphere or social fact is **INFERENCE**; the atmosphere, at least, is consistent everywhere she appears.

Male practitioners exist in the sources — *seiðmenn* — but overwhelmingly as saga villains, hired harmers, or figures of insult (sections 2 and 5). The professional, honoured, touring shape of the craft is female in every text that shows it whole.

### The graves — archaeology takes the stand

**ARCHAEOLOGY.** The strongest material evidence for the völva is the group of Viking Age burials — overwhelmingly wealthy female burials — containing **long, slender iron or wood-and-metal staffs** unlike any tool of daily life, generally interpreted as distaff-like ritual staffs: the völva's badge in the ground. Leszek Gardeła's work on the staff graves is the modern reference. Two graves anchor the discussion:

- **Fyrkat, Denmark.** Within the ring-fortress of Fyrkat (Harald Bluetooth's era, late tenth century), a woman was buried in a wagon body with goods of exceptional richness — and an assemblage that reads like Þorbjörg's pouch opened on a table: an iron staff; a box brooch containing **henbane seeds**; an **owl pellet**; small amulets and unusual animal remains; a silver toe-ring; fine imported ornaments. The Fyrkat woman is the single most-discussed "völva grave" in archaeology, and the combination — staff plus psychoactive seeds plus curated oddments — is why.
- **Oseberg, Norway.** The great ship burial of 834 contained, among its almost absurd wealth, a **staff** and a small pouch of **cannabis seeds** among the younger woman's goods. Both identifications come with caveats — the Oseberg staff's ritual character is argued from form and company, and seed identifications in century-old excavation records deserve humility — and honest scholarship (including the caveats) is the correct way to cite Oseberg: suggestive, famous, not decisive alone.

**ARCHAEOLOGY** (the pattern). Beyond the two famous graves, staff-like objects occur in female burials across Denmark, Norway, Sweden, and the Norse Atlantic, frequently accompanied by other "special" deposits: amulet rings, miniature objects, exotic imports, unusual animal bone, weighing equipment, and in several cases psychoactive plant remains. No single grave proves its occupant performed seiðr. The *pattern* — staffs recurring in rich women's graves with ritual-looking assemblages, matching a literary figure whose very name is "staff-bearer," carrying a charm-pouch, honoured and wealthy — is as close as archaeology gets to naming a profession. Scholars who accept "völva graves" as a category (Gardeła argues the case in detail) do so on this convergence, and the convergence, labelled honestly, is strong **INFERENCE** built on solid **ARCHAEOLOGY**.

### Henbane, cannabis, and the drug hypothesis

**INFERENCE**, plainly labelled. Henbane (*Hyoscyamus niger*) is a powerful deliriant — scopolamine and hyoscyamine — known across pre-modern Europe as a sorcerer's plant, capable of producing sensations of flight, transformation, and communion with the dead. Its seeds in the Fyrkat grave, beside a staff, invite the hypothesis that some völvas used psychoactive preparations to enter the working state. The hypothesis is respectable and unprovable: seeds can be medicine, amulet, or offering; no residue analysis has demonstrated seiðr-specific use; and the saga scenes — including the fullest one — show the working done with song, feasting, and sleep, with **no drug mentioned at all** (ATTESTED absence, as far as it goes). The sober position: psychoactives were present in the völva's material world, may plausibly have been part of some workings, and cannot be claimed as the engine of seiðr on present evidence. Any modern practice that centres drugs is centring a guess.

## 4. The Working Itself, As Far As It Can Be Reconstructed

Assemble every attested staging detail across the sources and a consistent shape emerges. This section presents that shape — and marks, at each step, where attestation ends.

### The staging

**ATTESTED** (composite, from Eiríks saga rauða, Örvar-Odds saga, Laxdæla saga, Friðþjófs saga):

1. **Invitation and arrival.** The völva is sent for or arrives on circuit. She does not simply begin; the community prepares.
2. **Feasting first.** A special meal precedes the working — in Eiríks saga, hearts of every available beast, and goat's-milk porridge. She works fed, honoured, at night.
3. **The platform.** The **seiðhjallr** is raised or prepared — a high seat, in Eiríks saga cushioned with hen feathers, indoors at the farm; outdoors in hostile workings (Kotkel). The practitioner is *lifted above the company* for the duration. This elevation is the single most consistent physical feature of seiðr across all the sources.
4. **The circle and the song.** Women (in Eiríks saga) form a ring around the platform and sing the **varðlokkur** — songs whose function, per the saga, is to *draw the spirits near*: Þorbjörg says explicitly that the powers who had shunned the working are now present because of the song. Song is not decoration on seiðr; in the sources it is its mechanism of summoning.
5. **The consultation.** The völva, from her seat, answers questions: Will the famine lift? When will the sickness pass? What of this marriage, this child, this voyage? Answers are given in the hearing of the household, in riddling or plain form.
6. **Gifts and departure.** Payment, thanks, and the tour moves on.

### What happened on the platform — trance, and its interpretations

**INFERENCE** (scholarly interpretation, Price prominently). The sources are curiously reticent about the völva's interior state during the working — Þorbjörg simply reports, afterward, what became clear. Later and hostile sources (Historia Norwegiae, some sagas) show seiðr-workers falling as if dead, yawning, or lying senseless while a spirit travels. Neil Price's influential reading gathers the whole complex as a **"performance of the mind"**: seiðr as enacted consciousness-work — trance, spirit-journey, and embodied performance fused, staged for an audience whose participation (the circle, the song, the held breath of the hall) is part of the technology. The reading is persuasive and widely used; it remains interpretation. What is ATTESTED is narrower: song summons, the seeress sits above, spirits approach, knowledge comes, answers issue.

### Spirit helpers — the gandir

**ATTESTED.** Seiðr does not work alone. The sources name its auxiliaries *gandir* (singular *gandr*) — spirits or powers summoned and sent, the same word-family as the *gandus* of the Historia Norwegiae, drawn by sound into a figure or sent abroad on errands. Þorbjörg's náttúrur ("natures") who must be coaxed near by song are the same dramatis personae in politer dress. **INFERENCE:** the gandir are best understood as the working familiars of the craft — the agency through which distant knowledge and distant harm were thought to travel.

### The uses: prophecy, weather, harm, and the rarity of healing

**ATTESTED** (saga pattern, consistent across independent texts):

- **Divination** is seiðr's honourable face: seasons, sickness, marriages, deaths, the outcomes of voyages and feuds. This is the völva's public profession.
- **Weather-working** is its most spectacular effect: storms raised at sea to drown an enemy (Kotkel; the Friðþjófs witches), winds bound or loosed. No other Norse art is credited with weather so regularly.
- **Harm at a distance** is its feared face: sickness, madness, torments, blighted luck, death — laid on named victims, often for hire, often in feud. Snorri's summary (taking wit and strength from one and giving to another) matches the saga case-load exactly.
- **Protection and healing** by seiðr are, by contrast, **rare in the sources** — and the guide says so plainly, because modern readers routinely assume a healing priestess the texts do not show. Healing in the sagas belongs more to leechcraft, to runes, to gods and charms; the völva's attested portfolio is knowledge and power, not therapy. A seiðkona may be consulted *about* a sickness (will it lift?) — that is divination, not cure.

### Neighbouring workings often confused with seiðr

- **Hamr-travel and shape-shifting. ATTESTED** as concepts (*hamrammr*, "shape-strong"; the *hamr* as the travelling form), and Odin is said in Ynglinga saga to change shape while his body lay as if asleep or dead — in a passage adjacent to, but distinguished from, the seiðr summary. Later folklore's flying witches descend partly from here. Careful scholarship keeps hamr-travel as seiðr's close cousin, not its synonym.
- **Útiseta** — "sitting out" at night, at crossroads or on howes, to gain hidden knowledge. **ATTESTED** mostly in later law codes (which forbid it) and folklore; saga attestations are thin. Label it: a real named practice of the North, late-documented, probably old, not demonstrably a part of formal seiðr.
- **Dream-work and waking the dead** — attested at seiðr's edges (Baldrs draumar above; prophetic dreams throughout the sagas usually arrive unbidden, which is spá's weather, not seiðr's craft).

## 5. Ergi and Gender — The Shame at the Heart of Seiðr

No honest guide can soften this section, because the sources do not: seiðr was **female-coded**, and for a man to practise it carried a specific, corrosive shame called **ergi**.

### The complex itself

**ATTESTED.** *Ergi* (adjective *argr*) is among the gravest insults in Old Norse: unmanliness with a sexual core — the imputation that a man has taken the passive, female role, in sex and by extension in any shameful receptivity. It belonged to the small class of insults (*níð*) that law treated as fighting words, answerable by blood. Snorri states the rule flatly: seiðr's ergi was so great that men were shamed by practising it, and the art was therefore taught to women. The Lokasenna taunt (section 2) shows the rule operating in the poetry's divine society: Odin drummed like a völva on Samsø, and Loki calls it *argr* to his face before the gods.

Why was seiðr unmanly? The sources never explain; scholars reconstruct (**INFERENCE**, several non-exclusive readings):

- **Receptivity.** The seiðr-worker sits still, is sung to, is entered and informed by spirits — a vessel, where the masculine ideal acts, strikes, and speaks first. Brit Solli's influential reading develops this: seiðr's whole posture transgresses the Norse gender order, in which to be *used* by powers is female, whatever the body's sex.
- **Association with seiðr's effects.** Seiðr works invisibly, at a distance, by indirection — the opposite of the open blow. Indirect power was already woman-coded in the heroic imagination (the weaving of spells beside the weaving of cloth).
- **The Vanir connection.** The art came from Freyja and the Vanir, the gods of sexuality and fertility, whose cult was entangled with transgressive rites in the sources' memory.

### Odin's paradox

**ATTESTED.** And yet the greatest single practitioner in the mythology is male, and is the All-Father. Odin learned seiðr from Freyja (Ynglinga), practised it on Samsø (Lokasenna), raises the dead, sits out, hangs, shape-shifts — and takes the insult for it, in Lokasenna, without the poem suggesting the charge is false. He simply absorbs the shame as the price of knowledge, as he absorbs every price: an eye, hanging, wounds. **INFERENCE** (a standard scholarly reading): Odin is the exception that proves the rule — the god of ecstasy, poetry, death, and boundary-crossing, whose masculinity is not diminished by the transgression because transgression is his office. Mortal men had no such licence, and the sagas' seiðmenn pay for the art with their reputations and usually their lives.

### Seiðmenn in the sagas

**ATTESTED** (pattern). The male seiðr-workers of the family sagas — Kotkel and his sons, Þorgrímr nef, the seiðmenn of the legendary material — are remembered as foreign, sinister, hireling, or doomed, and their workings as attacks. Not one receives the völva's honoured staging: feast, high seat, gifts, gratitude. Whether this reflects Viking Age reality or Christian-era saga morality is honestly **unresolvable from the texts alone** — but the *memory* the texts preserve is unanimous: a woman with a staff was a pillar of the world's order, feared and fed; a man with the same songs was a scandal. Modern readers building a gentle, genderless seiðr are building against the sources, not from them.

### A note on the scholarly debate

Serious study of this material runs through Strömbäck (1935) to Solli's *Seid: Myter om det nordiske* (2002), Else Mundal's work on the völva's literary construction, and Price's performance reading — and it includes live disagreement about how far the *ergi* complex describes ritual reality versus literary ideology, and about how the sagas' female practitioners were reimagined by their Christian authors. This guide has presented the consensus core (female coding, male shame, Odin the exception) as attested in the texts; the *explanations* of the core are INFERENCE and remain argued. That is the correct state of the field, and the guide leaves it there.

## 6. Law, Suppression, and the Long Afterlife

**ATTESTED.** When Christianity came to the North, seiðr was among the practices the new order named and banned. The early Norwegian Christian law sections (the *kristinréttr* material preserved in the Gulathing and Frostathing traditions) and Icelandic law (*Grágás*) penalise sorcery, seiðr, and útiseta; to "wake trolls" or practise the old arts draws outlawry-grade consequences in the codes' severe clauses. Church writing of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries treats seiðr as diabolical by definition — the saga scenes of evil seiðmenn are partly this doctrine wearing narrative clothes. The legal record matters doubly: a law is evidence the practice (or the fear of it) was alive enough to forbid, and the ferocity of the forbidding helped *create* the saga cliché of the sorcerer-villain. Reading the sources means reading through that filter, always.

**Folklore (labelled as such).** Seiðr did not so much die as sink. In later Icelandic and Norwegian folklore, figures recognisably descended from the völva and the seiðmaðr persist for centuries: night-sitters at crossroads, sendings and fetch-spirits, weather-raising wise-women, cunning men with books of signs. Iceland's early-modern witch trials (seventeenth century, burning mostly *men* — a striking reversal of the mainland pattern, and of the old gender coding) drew on learned continental demonology as much as on old seiðr. Folklore preserves seiðr's *silhouette* — the platform becomes the howe and the crossroads, the gandir become sendings — but folklore is not a time capsule, and this guide cites it only as afterlife, never as evidence for Viking Age practice.

## 7. What We Do Not Know

This is the most important section of the guide, and the shortest to write, because the honest catalogue of ignorance is exact:

- **No melodies survive.** Not one note of a varðlokk. The "ward-songs" were the engine of the working, and their music is wholly lost. Any recording sold as "authentic völva chant" is composition, not recovery.
- **No ritual script survives.** The order of a working — preparation, words, gestures, durations, closings — is unknown beyond the staging outline of section 4. Eiríks saga gives a scene, not a liturgy.
- **No curriculum survives.** How a völva was chosen, taught, or initiated; whether the craft ran in families of sisters (Þorbjörg is the *last of nine* — the detail haunts the field); what the charm-pouch held beyond the saga's word *taufr*: unknown.
- **The interior is unrecoverable.** What the working felt like from the seat — trance, possession, performed concentration, or something our categories miss entirely — no text says, because no practitioner ever wrote. Price's "performance of the mind" is the best modern name for a darkness.
- **The words are lost.** No seiðr charm-text survives in situ. Galdr leaves metrical traces in poetry; seiðr's sung summons was probably improvisational, formulaic, or secret — or simply unrecorded because its singers could not write and its enemies would not preserve it.

A guide that filled these gaps smoothly would be lying to you in the places where the truth is most interesting. The gaps *are* the finding: seiðr was an oral, embodied, initiatory craft, and oral embodied crafts die with their practitioners. What conversion could not burn, silence finished.

## 8. Modern Seiðr — Reconstruction, Honourably Named

**RECONSTRUCTION** — this whole section, by its nature.

From the late twentieth century onward, practitioners within modern Heathenry and Norse paganism — the Old Ways, revived under many banners, among them the Heathen Third Path — have rebuilt a working seiðr from the fragments this guide has catalogued: the platform raised again, varðlokkur composed anew for the circle, the staff taken up, questions asked of the hidden on behalf of communities. Some modern seiðr is showy; some is disciplined, source-drunk, and scrupulous about exactly the labels used here. The best of it has one defining virtue: **it calls itself reconstruction.** It knows the melodies are new compositions, the liturgies new buildings on old foundations, the initiations new-made.

The dishonest version exists too — lineages claiming unbroken Viking Age transmission, "ancient" techniques first printed in the 1980s, initiations into secrets no medieval text contains. Against those, this guide's position is the one the evidence forces: there is nothing shameful in reconstruction — every living tradition was somebody's reconstruction once — and everything shameful in dressing it as survival. A modern seiðkona who says "I built this from Eiríks saga and the Fyrkat grave, and the rest is mine" stands closer to Þorbjörg than any claimant to a secret unbroken line, because Þorbjörg's own world ran on honesty about what the song was for.

If you practise: build from the sources, label your building, honour the dead women whose names we mostly do not have, and let the gaps stay gaps. They are holy in their way. They are what two hundred years of silence left us, and silence, too, is information.

## Appendix A: A Small Lexicon of Seiðr

For readers working with translations, these are the load-bearing words, with their honest weights:

- **seiðr** — the art and the event: fate-working magic performed with song from a raised seat. Etymology debated (section 1). The word survives into modern Icelandic *seiður*, still meaning sorcery — the term never entirely died, even when the practice did.
- **síða** (verb) — to perform seiðr. Loki's word for what Odin did on Samsø.
- **efla seið** — "to perform, to strengthen seiðr": the saga phrase for mounting a working.
- **völva** — staff-bearer; the professional prophetess-practitioner. Plural *völvur*.
- **seiðkona / seiðmaðr** — seiðr-woman / seiðr-man.
- **spákona / spámaðr** — prophetess / prophet; the diviner as such.
- **seiðhjallr** — the seiðr-platform or high seat; the craft's one indispensable piece of furniture.
- **varðlokkur** (also read *varðlokur*) — the "ward-songs": the summoning songs sung to draw the spirits to the working. From *vörðr*, a guardian or ward — the songs are, literally, guardian-callers.
- **gandr / gandir** — the spirit-helper(s) summoned and sent; also the travelling fetch of a worker.
- **náttúrur** — "natures": Þorbjörg's word in Eiríks saga for the powers drawn near by the song.
- **taufr** — charms, amulets; the contents of the völva's belt-pouch, and in later usage, sorcery generally.
- **ergi / argr** — unmanliness, the shame-complex of section 5; the gravest insult-register in the language.
- **fjölkyngi** — "much-knowing": learned magic in the broad sense.
- **útiseta** — "sitting out": night-sitting for hidden knowledge, late-attested, legally banned.
- **hamr** — the shape or form a traveller of the mind could wear; *hamrammr*, shape-strong.

Each of these words is a small excavation. They survive; their referents mostly do not — which is the entire subject of this guide in miniature.

## Appendix B: How to Read Any Seiðr Claim — A Pocket Method

Because seiðr attracts invention the way the platform attracted spirits, this guide closes its teaching with a method any reader can apply to any claim, in any book, video, or course — including claims made by people with staffs, followings, and fees:

1. **Ask for the source.** A claim about ancient seiðr that cannot name its text, grave, or law clause is modern opinion wearing a cloak. "The völvas believed…" without a citation is a red flag in a single sentence.
2. **Ask for the date.** A thirteenth-century saga scene, a tenth-century grave, and a seventeenth-century folklore tale are three different kinds of evidence about three different centuries. Collapsing them into "the Viking Age" is how inventions are built.
3. **Ask the four-label question.** Attested, archaeology, inference, or reconstruction? An honest teacher answers without flinching. A dishonest one changes the subject to lineage, initiation, or the fee.
4. **Check the silent places.** The melodies, the liturgy, the curriculum, the interior — section 7's catalogue. Any system that has suddenly *filled* one of those silences has filled it from the claimant's imagination. Imagination is not a crime; unlabelled imagination sold as antiquity is.
5. **Watch for the Ibn Fadlan error and its cousins.** Miscited eyewitnesses (section 2), drugs promoted from hypothesis to engine (section 3), healing promoted from rarity to centre (section 4), gender quietly deleted (section 5) — these are the four standard moves by which fragment becomes fantasy. You now know all four.
6. **Respect the reconstruction that names itself.** The modern worker who builds from Eiríks saga and the staff graves, and says so, is doing the most honourable thing the evidence allows. Support that honesty wherever you find it; it is rarer than it should be, and it is the only road by which a living practice can grow from these fragments without lying about its roots.

The sources give us a woman on a high seat in a hungry Greenland winter, singing-power drawn near by a Christian girl's voice, answering for the spring. Everything beyond that scene is either labelled inference or silence. Hold fast to the scene, label the inference, honour the silence — and you will understand seiðr better than nine-tenths of what is published under its name.
