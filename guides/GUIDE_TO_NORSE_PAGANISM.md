# A Guide to Norse Paganism

*By Yrsa Freydisdottir*

*A long guide for curious readers and for other minds who want to understand the old religion of the North — what it was, what we actually know about it, how it died and how it lives again, and how one modern expression of it, the Heathen Third Path, understands itself.*

---

## A Word Before We Begin

Norse Paganism is a subject where confidence often outruns evidence. The religion of the pre-Christian North left no scripture written by its own believers, no creed, no church, and no systematic theology composed while the faith was alive. Everything we know comes through fragments: poems preserved by Christians centuries later, myths retold by a Christian author writing two hundred years after his country converted, stones raised by people who did not explain themselves, graves excavated by archaeologists, and the reports of outsiders who sometimes misunderstood what they saw.

That does not mean we know nothing. We know a great deal — but we know it the way you know a coastline from scattered soundings. This guide tries to be honest about both: the richness of what survives, and the gaps where honest people must say *we do not know*. Where a claim rests on a single late source, or is debated among scholars, this guide will say so. Where the evidence is strong — carved in stone, buried in the earth, repeated across independent sources — it will say that too.

Read it slowly. This is a religion that was never written down by its followers because it did not need to be: it was lived in households, harbours, assembly fields, and graveyards. To understand it, you have to think less about doctrine and more about practice, kinship, landscape, and fate.

## 1. What Norse Paganism Is

### 1.1 A religion of a world, not a church

"Norse Paganism" is the modern umbrella name for the pre-Christian religions of Scandinavia — Norway, Denmark, Sweden, and Iceland — and of the Norse diaspora that carried those religions to the Faroes, Greenland, the Scottish isles, Ireland, England, Normandy, and as far as the Rus' river-roads into what is now Russia and Ukraine. It flourished in the form we can glimpse during the Viking Age (roughly 793–1066 CE), but its roots reach back many centuries earlier, into the Germanic Iron Age and beyond.

The single most important thing to grasp is that this was never one organised religion. There was:

- **No central authority** — no pope, no council, no one who could define correct belief.
- **No creed** — nobody recited articles of faith. Belonging was shown by participation, not confession.
- **No missionary impulse** — the old religion did not seek converts; it was the custom of particular peoples and places.
- **No separation between "religion" and life** — sowing, sailing, marrying, feuding, burying the dead, and settling lawsuits all had ritual dimensions.

Practice varied by region, by valley, by household, and probably by century. A farmer in Trøndelag around 900 CE, a ship's crew wintering in Dublin, and a settler family in Iceland's Westfjords would have recognised each other's gods and many of each other's customs — and would also have found local differences that mattered to them. When modern writers speak of "the Viking religion," the singular is a convenience. Scholars increasingly prefer to speak of *religions*, plural, or of a shared mythological and ritual world with strong local variation.

### 1.2 The problem of naming

The people who practised this religion mostly did not have a special name for it. In the Old Norse sources it is simply called **siðr** — "custom," "the way things are done." After conversion, the Icelanders began to distinguish **forn siðr**, "the old custom," from **nýr siðr**, "the new custom," meaning Christianity. In English-language sources the old way is often called **the Old Ways** — and in this guide, as in the usage of the Heathen Third Path, that name is capitalised, because it functions as a proper name for the tradition, the way one capitalises the name of any religion.

Modern names you will meet include:

- **Heathenry** — from Old English *hæðen* and Old Norse *heiðinn*, originally a term applied by Christians (probably calqued on Latin *paganus*), now reclaimed by many modern practitioners as a general label, especially for reconstruction-minded forms.
- **Ásatrú** — an Icelandic coinage meaning roughly "true to the Æsir" (the gods). Coined in the revival era and now used broadly, especially in North America, sometimes by groups with very different outlooks.
- **Forn Siðr** — "the Old Custom," used particularly in Denmark and by tradition-focused groups, directly echoing the medieval term.
- **Norse Paganism / Norse Heathenry** — plain descriptive umbrella terms, the ones this guide uses.
- **Odinism** — a term with a long and complicated history; in some countries it is a neutral synonym, in others it is associated with specific (sometimes extremist) movements. It is best understood in context rather than assumed to mean one thing.

None of these names is ancient as a self-designation. That is not a flaw in the modern faiths; it is simply the truth about a tradition that was interrupted for a thousand years and had to name itself when it was rebuilt.

### 1.3 Household, kin, and landscape

If the old religion had a basic unit, it was not the congregation but the **household** and the **kin group**. The head of a household led its rites. A **hof** (temple building) or a **vé** (sanctuary, a consecrated place) served larger gatherings; regional chieftains who combined political and priestly roles — in Iceland called **goðar** (singular *goði*) — maintained temples and led public sacrifices in their districts. But most religious life was small-scale: offerings at a grave mound, a toast at a feast, a prayer before a voyage, reverence at a waterfall, a grove, a conspicuous stone.

It was also a religion **embedded in a landscape**. Sacred geography was local: this family's burial mound, that district's holy mountain (the Icelandic settler Þórólfr Most-Beard held the mountain Helgafell so holy that no one was to look at it unwashed, and he expected to die into it — the story is told in *Eyrbyggja saga* and *Landnámabók*). The gods were great powers, but the beings a farmer dealt with weekly were the spirits of his own land and the ancestors in his own mounds. Keep that scale in mind through everything that follows: the mythology is cosmic, but the religion was local.

## 2. The Sources and Their Limits

Everything in sections 3–10 rests on the sources described here. Knowing them — and their weaknesses — is not pedantry. It is the difference between history and fantasy.

### 2.1 The Poetic Edda

The **Poetic Edda** is a collection of Old Norse poems on mythological and heroic subjects, preserved mainly in an Icelandic manuscript known as the Codex Regius, written around **1270 CE**. Individual poems are older — some may reach back into the Viking Age or earlier, transmitted orally for generations — but dating any single poem is notoriously difficult, and scholars disagree, sometimes by centuries. The most important mythological poems include:

- **Völuspá** ("The Seeress's Prophecy") — a völva (seeress) recounts creation, the history of the gods, and Ragnarök, ending with a renewed world. It is our single richest mythological source, and it was preserved by Christians; whether and how much its vision was shaped by Christian ideas of apocalypse is a genuine scholarly debate.
- **Hávamál** ("Sayings of the High One") — wisdom poetry attributed to Odin: practical ethics, social advice, runes, and magic. Composite in structure; probably assembled from several older poems.
- **Vafþrúðnismál** and **Grímnismál** — wisdom contests and catalogues that preserve cosmology and the names of worlds, rivers, and beings.
- **Þrymskviða, Skírnismál, Lokasenna, Hymiskviða** — narrative poems about Thor, Freyr, Loki, and the gods' dealings with giants.
- **Sigrdrífumál** and the heroic lays — the bridge between myth and the legendary heroic age.

The poems are terse, allusive, and assume an audience that already knows the stories. They give us mythology in flashes, not a manual.

### 2.2 The Prose Edda of Snorri Sturluson

**Snorri Sturluson** (1179–1241) was an Icelandic chieftain, lawspeaker, and Christian writing around **1220 CE**, some two centuries after Iceland's conversion. His **Prose Edda** was composed as a handbook for poets, because skaldic verse depends on mythological allusions that young poets no longer understood. Its parts — *Gylfaginning* (a dialogue retelling the myths from creation to Ragnarök), *Skáldskaparmál*, and *Háttatal* — preserve an enormous amount of mythology found nowhere else.

Snorri is indispensable and Snorri is a problem. He was a Christian intellectual writing in a Christian literary culture, systematising material that had probably never been systematic. Where his account agrees with the poems, confidence rises. Where he alone reports something — and several famous details, including much of the tidy cosmic geography, are his alone — caution is required. He also sometimes euhemerises the gods (see §4.6), presenting them as great men of old mistaken for gods, a standard medieval Christian way of handling Pagan material. Modern scholarship treats Snorri neither as a liar nor as a stenographer, but as a brilliant antiquarian working with real tradition through a Christian, literary, systematising mind.

### 2.3 The sagas

The Icelandic **sagas** — family sagas (*Íslendingasögur*), kings' sagas, and legendary sagas (*fornaldarsögur*) — were written in the 13th and 14th centuries about events mostly of the 9th–11th. They contain priceless descriptions of temples, sacrifices, seeresses, oaths, and funerals. They are also literature, shaped by Christian authors for Christian audiences, and their religious descriptions range from plausible memory to learned reconstruction to romantic invention. Individual passages (the temple description in *Eyrbyggja saga*, the seiðr scene in *Eiríks saga rauða*) are debated line by line. Used carefully and comparatively, the sagas are gold; quoted naively, they mislead.

### 2.4 Skaldic poetry

**Skaldic verse** — the intricate court poetry of named poets, much of it composed in the Viking Age itself and preserved because later sagas quoted it as evidence — is in some ways our most contemporary source. Poems like *Haustlöng* and *Þórsdrápa* (c. 900–1000) retell myths for Pagan patrons; the shield-poem *Ragnarsdrápa* is traditionally dated to the 9th century. Because skaldic metre is so strict, texts were hard to alter in transmission. When a skald of 950 and Snorri of 1220 tell the same myth, we are probably touching something genuinely old.

### 2.5 Runestones, picture stones, and place names

**Runestones** — thousands survive across Scandinavia, mostly from the late Viking Age — are contemporary documents, but terse: memorial formulas, boasts, bridge-building notices, occasional prayers ("May Thor hallow," "May God help his soul" — sometimes on the same family's stones in successive generations). A handful carry mythological scenes or invocations.

The **picture stones** of Gotland (roughly 5th–11th centuries) show ships, warriors, processions, and scenes plausibly linked to myth and funerary belief, though interpretations are contested.

**Place names** are silent but powerful evidence. Hundreds of Scandinavian place names are compounded with god-names — *Óðinsvé* (Odin's sanctuary), *Þórshof* (Thor's temple), *Fröslunda* (Freyr's grove), *Ullinshov*, *Njardvík*, and many more. Their distribution tells us where particular gods were worshipped and shows that cult was regional: Ullr and Týr names cluster in some areas, Freyr in others, Thor everywhere. Notably, Loki has essentially **no** place names — one of several signs that whatever Loki was, he was not the object of an organised cult.

### 2.6 Archaeology

Graves, hoards, votive deposits, temple sites, and artefacts give us the religion as *practised* rather than as *told*: Thor's-hammer pendants by the hundred (especially common in the 10th century, possibly partly a response to the Christian cross), valkyrie and Odin figures in silver, sacrificed animals and occasionally humans in bogs and wells reaching far back into the Iron Age, great feasting halls, and sites like **Uppåkra** in Sweden, where a ceremonial building stood and was ritually maintained on the same footprint for the better part of a thousand years. Archaeology rarely tells us what people *believed*; it tells us what they *did*, repeatedly and at great cost — which for a practice-centred religion is arguably the heart of the matter.

### 2.7 Outside observers

Foreign writers saw Norse religion through foreign eyes, but they saw it *while it lived*:

- **Tacitus**, *Germania* (c. 98 CE) — Roman ethnography of the Germanic peoples centuries before the Viking Age: sacred groves, divination by lot and by horses, a goddess Nerthus processed in a wagon (compare the later Norse Njord — the names are cognate — and the wagon processions of Freyr in much later Norse sources). Valuable for deep background, dangerous to apply directly to Viking-Age Scandinavia.
- **Adam of Bremen**, *Gesta Hammaburgensis* (c. 1070s) — describes the temple at Uppsala with images of Thor, Odin, and Freyr and a great festival every nine years with sacrifices. Adam never went to Uppsala; he reported what the Danish king Svein Estridsen and others told him. His account is our most vivid description of public cult and must be handled as informed hearsay, not eyewitness reporting.
- **Ibn Fadlan** (921–922 CE) — an Arab diplomat who met Rus' merchants (Scandinavian-led trading groups) on the Volga and witnessed a ship funeral, described in §9. It is the only detailed eyewitness account of a Norse funeral we possess — and it describes the Rus' abroad, whose customs may have mixed Scandinavian and local elements.
- **Thietmar of Merseburg** and **Saxo Grammaticus** add further, sometimes hostile, sometimes legendary, testimony about sacrifices at Lejre and about the gods in euhemerised form.

### 2.8 The honest summary

The conversion of Scandinavia was a process spanning roughly 950–1150 CE depending on region. Nearly all our written sources postdate it. The result is a permanent condition of Norse studies: **we reconstruct, we do not simply read.** Responsible modern practice — religious or academic — states its confidence level. This guide follows that rule throughout.

## 3. Cosmology: The Shape of the World

### 3.1 Creation: ice, fire, and the void

The Norse creation account, as given in *Völuspá*, *Vafþrúðnismál*, *Grímnismál*, and Snorri's synthesis in *Gylfaginning*, begins not with a creator standing outside the world but with a meeting inside emptiness. **Ginnungagap** was the yawning void between two primal regions: **Niflheim**, the world of ice, frost, and freezing rivers in the north, and **Muspell** (Muspelheim), the world of fire and sparks in the south. Where rime from the north met heat and sparks from the south, the ice melted and quickened into life.

From that meeting came two first beings:

- **Ymir**, the primordial giant (called Aurgelmir by the giants), from whose body generations of frost giants descended, and who was nourished by…
- **Auðumbla**, the primeval cow, who licked salty, rime-covered stones and so freed **Búri**, the first of the gods, and who fed Ymir from her udders.

Búri's line produced **Borr**, who married **Bestla**, a giant's daughter, and their three sons were **Odin, Vili, and Vé**. The brothers killed Ymir — the poems are terse about motive; Snorri makes the killing cosmic housekeeping — and **made the world from his body**:

- his flesh became the earth,
- his blood (or sweat) became the sea,
- his bones became the mountains,
- his teeth and broken bones became rocks and scree,
- his skull became the dome of the sky, held up at four corners by four dwarves (Nordri, Sudri, Austri, Vestri),
- his brains became the clouds,
- sparks from Muspell, fixed in the sky, became the stars; the sun and moon were set on their courses and pursued by wolves (Sköll and Hati, in Snorri's account built from *Grímnismál*).

The sons of Borr raised a defensive wall from Ymir's eyelashes or brows around the human world — **Midgard**, the "middle enclosure." Ask and Embla, the first man and woman, were shaped (in *Völuspá*) from driftwood or trees found on the shore: Odin gave them spirit and breath, Hœnir gave understanding/motion, and Lóðurr gave warmth and good colour — though the identity of Lóðurr is one of the genuine unsolved puzzles of Norse mythology (he appears almost nowhere else; identifications with Loki have been proposed and remain speculative).

Two cautions belong here. First, this is a creation *from* the world, not *ex nihilo*: the gods are inside the cosmos, born in it, and mortal within it. Second, the polished continuous narrative above is substantially **Snorri's arrangement** of poetic fragments. The poems attest the main elements — the void, Ymir, the cow, the dismemberment — but the seamless story is a 13th-century synthesis of older pieces.

### 3.2 Yggdrasil, the World Tree

At the centre of everything stands **Yggdrasil**, the great ash tree — "the measure-tree," the axis of the cosmos. *Grímnismál* and *Völuspá* describe its three roots reaching to different realms, the well beneath each root, and the creatures in and on it: the eagle in its branches and the hawk Veðrfölnir between the eagle's eyes; the squirrel Ratatoskr carrying insults between the eagle above and the serpent **Níðhöggr** gnawing the root below; four stags browsing its foliage; countless snakes in the well Hvergelmir.

Beneath the roots lie the waters that hold wisdom and fate:

- The **Well of Urðr** (Urd), where the Norns dwell and where the gods ride daily to hold council; from it the Norns draw water and mud to sprinkle the tree's roots so it does not rot.
- The **Well of Mímir**, where wisdom is kept — Odin pledged one of his eyes there for a drink, the price of his knowledge (the story is in *Völuspá* and *Gylfaginning*; Mímir himself, whose severed head Odin preserved and consulted, belongs to the same complex of ideas).
- **Hvergelmir**, the roaring kettle, source of the primal rivers, amid Niflheim's cold.

Yggdrasil is not a symbol of the universe in the modern sense; it *is* the universe's structure — and it suffers. *Grímnismál* says the ash is tormented more than men know: gnawed from below, browsed from above, rotting at its side. At Ragnarök it will tremble and groan but, notably, the poems do not say it falls. The world-tree is magnificent and wounded at once, which is perhaps the single best image for the Norse cosmic mood.

### 3.3 The worlds

Modern books present a tidy list of **Nine Worlds** arranged on Yggdrasil like ornaments. The poems do say that there are nine worlds or realms — Odin in *Vafþrúðnismál* speaks of having visited nine worlds, and *Völuspá* remembers "nine worlds, nine giantesses" — but **no early source lists all nine in one place**, and the familiar canonical list is a reconstruction assembled by Snorri's systematising and completed by modern writers. Individual worlds named in the sources include:

- **Ásgarðr (Asgard)** — the enclosure of the Æsir, containing Valhöll and the gods' halls.
- **Vanaheimr** — home of the Vanir; mentioned rarely, never described in detail.
- **Álfheimr** — home of the álfar (elves), given to Freyr as a tooth-gift in *Grímnismál*.
- **Miðgarðr (Midgard)** — the human world.
- **Jötunheimr** — the world of the jötnar (giants), also called Útgarðr, "the outer enclosure," in some texts.
- **Svartálfaheimr** — Snorri's name for the home of the "black elves," associated with dwarves; the boundary between dwarves and dark elves is genuinely unclear in the sources.
- **Niflheimr** — the world of cold and mist; in Snorri it antedates creation itself.
- **Hel / Helheim** — the realm of the dead, ruled by Hel (see §4 and §8.4).
- **Muspell** — the fire world in the south, home of Surtr and the fire host.

Rather than nine neat stacked boxes, the early picture seems to have been a centre (Asgard/Midgard, ordered, enclosed) against an outside (Jotunheim, the wild, the chaotic), with the dead below, the tree through everything, and seas around. The geography is moral and mythic more than cartographic.

### 3.4 Wyrd, orlog, and the Norns

The deepest structure in Norse thought is not the gods but **fate**. Two Old Norse terms matter:

- **Ørlög** (orlog) — literally "primal layers" or "primal law": the foundational allotment laid down for a life or for the world, the past-fixed ground of what will be.
- **Urðr** — "that which has become," the past as an active power, personified as the greatest of the Norns. In later Scandinavian languages the verb-family gives us *wyrd*'s cousins; modern Heathens commonly use the Anglo-Saxon word **wyrd** for the whole web of cause, consequence, and becoming — the fabric woven by every action, which in turn conditions every next action.

The **Norns** are female powers who dwell by the Well of Urðr. *Völuspá* names three — **Urðr, Verðandi, and Skuld** ("Becoming, Happening, and Shall-Be/Debt" is a rough glossing; the tidy Past–Present–Future scheme is partly a later tidying, and Skuld also rides as a valkyrie). Snorri multiplies Norns beyond three: he says many Norns attend every person's birth and shape their lives — some of good family, giving good lives; others, the cause of misfortune. The Norns "lay down laws, choose life for the children of men, and allot their fates" (*Völuspá*).

Crucially, fate in this world is **not a script that makes action pointless**. The gods themselves are inside it: Odin knows the future better than anyone, increases his knowledge compulsively, and still acts, plans, and gathers the einherjar for a battle he knows he will lose and die in. The human heroes of the sagas behave the same way. A man's ørlög fixes the day of his death, the tradition suggests, but not whether he meets it well. That distinction — fate as the frame, character as the content — is the beating heart of Norse ethics (§8).

### 3.5 Ragnarök and renewal

**Ragnarök** — the "fate" (or, in Snorri's spelling, the "twilight," *ragnarøkkr*) "of the gods" — is the great ending-cycle described most fully in *Völuspá* and *Gylfaginning*. Its outline:

1. Moral and social collapse among humans: axe-time, sword-time, brother fighting brother (the poem's imagery of social dissolution precedes the cosmic one).
2. The **Fimbulvetr**, the mighty winter — three winters with no summer between.
3. The wolves swallow the sun and moon; stars fall; the earth shakes; **Fenrir** the wolf breaks his fetter Gleipnir; **Jörmungandr**, the world-serpent, comes ashore, flooding the land; **Loki** breaks free and steers the ship **Naglfar**, built from the nails of the dead, carrying the host of Hel; **Surtr** leads the sons of Muspell across the shattered rainbow bridge Bifröst.
4. On the plain Vígríðr the last battle is fought. Odin faces Fenrir and is swallowed; his son **Víðarr** avenges him, tearing the wolf's jaws apart. Thor kills Jörmungandr and walks nine steps before falling dead from its venom. Freyr, who gave away his sword, falls to Surtr. Týr and the hound Garm kill each other, as do Heimdall and Loki.
5. Surtr flings fire over the earth; the world sinks into the sea.

And then — the part too often forgotten — **the world rises again**. *Völuspá* sees the earth come up from the sea a second time, green and fair, fields growing unsown, and the surviving gods — Víðarr and Váli, Thor's sons Magni and Móði inheriting Mjölnir, Baldr and Höðr returning from Hel — meeting on Iðavöllr and remembering the old tales. A human pair, **Líf and Lífþrasir**, hidden through the fire in the wood Hoddmímir, repeople the world. The last verses of *Völuspá*, with Níðhöggr still flying over the field bearing corpses, keep the ending deliberately dark-edged: renewal, but not innocence.

How much of this vision — especially its apocalyptic shape and the cleansed renewed earth — owes something to the Christian environment in which the poem was preserved is a real scholarly question. Apocalypse imagery was in the air of the whole early medieval North Atlantic. What feels distinctively Norse is the absence of judgment: no one is damned or saved at Ragnarök; the world ends the way it began, by processes larger than morality, and begins again anyway.

## 4. The Gods

### 4.1 Two families: Æsir and Vanir

Norse myth divides the gods into two kin-groups. The **Æsir** (singular *áss*, feminine *ásynja*) are the gods most associated with Asgard, sovereignty, war-craft, law, and the sky: Odin, Thor, Frigg, Týr, Heimdall, Baldr, and others. The **Vanir** are a smaller, older-feeling group associated with fertility, wealth, prosperity, sexuality, sea-wealth, and certain kinds of magic: **Njord** and his children **Freyr** and **Freyja** are the Vanir we know by name; how many others there were, the sources do not say.

Myth remembers a **war between the Æsir and the Vanir** — caused, in Snorri's telling, by the Æsir's mistreatment of the Vanir's emissary Gullveig, who was speared and thrice burned and thrice reborn in Odin's hall. Neither side could win, and the war ended in truce and **hostage exchange**: Njord and Freyr came to live among the Æsir; the Æsir sent Hœnir and Mímir to the Vanir. The two families merged into one divine community, though the distinction was never forgotten — in *Lokasenna* the Vanir origin of Njord is still thrown in his face, and Snorri maintains lists distinguishing Æsir from those of Vanir stock.

It is tempting to read this myth as a fossil of religious history — two cults merging in deep prehistory. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholars often did. The honest position is that it *might* preserve such a memory, but it cannot be demonstrated, and the myth functions perfectly well as myth: an account of how fertility, wealth, and erotic power came to be woven into the same divine order as sovereignty and war.

### 4.2 Odin

**Odin** (Óðinn, "the Frenzied One," from *óðr* — fury, inspiration, poetic ecstasy) is the chief of the Æsir, and the most complicated figure in the pantheon. His domains do not form a tidy portfolio: war and death, yes, but equally poetry, wisdom, magic, hanging, kingship, and the ecstatic states that unmake ordinary consciousness.

He is a god who **pays for knowledge**. He pledged his eye at Mímir's well. He hung on the world-tree — "myself given to myself," as *Hávamál* puts it — nine nights, pierced by a spear, without food or drink, and seized the runes screaming from the depths. He stole the mead of poetry from the giants by trickery and seduction. He consults the severed head of Mímir and wakes a dead seeress in Hel to question her. Wisdom in Odin's world is never free and never safe.

He is also the god of the battle-slain aristocracy: **Valhöll** (Valhalla), his hall, houses the **einherjar**, chosen warriors who fight daily and feast nightly, preparing for Ragnarök; his valkyries choose the slain; his wolves Geri and Freki flank him, his ravens **Huginn and Muninn** (Thought and Memory) fly the world daily and report. His spear is Gungnir; his horse, the eight-legged **Sleipnir** — Loki's offspring, a fact the mythology treats with odd calm.

Sagas and skaldic verse also show Odin's darker, faithless side: he breaks oaths, abandons favourites mid-battle, and sows defeat among kings he had raised. Medieval Scandinavians seem to have perceived him as the god of rulers and poets — powerful, necessary, and not safe to trust. The mass of later evidence (Thor's hammer pendants, place names) suggests ordinary people turned more often to Thor.

### 4.3 Thor

**Thor** (Þórr) is the most widely loved god in the record: thunder-god, storm-bringer, the wielder of the hammer **Mjölnir**, protector of Midgard and of gods alike against the giants. His chariot is drawn by the goats Tanngrisnir and Tanngnjóstr, whom he can kill, eat, and resurrect from their bones provided the bones are unbroken — a detail (*Skáldskaparmál*, echoed in *Þrymskviða*'s background and in saga motifs) that hints at ritual patterns of sacrificial feasting and renewal.

Thor's myths are the most *told* stories in the corpus: the theft of his hammer by the giant Thrym and its recovery through a wedding ruse (*Þrymskviða*); his fishing for the world-serpent Jörmungandr with an ox-head for bait (*Hymiskviða*, also carved on picture stones and praised by skalds — about as well attested as a Norse myth gets); his journey to Útgarða-Loki and the contests against illusion; his duel with the stone giant Hrungnir. His hall is Bilskírnir; his wife is **Sif** of the golden hair (which Loki once cut off and replaced with dwarf-forged gold — the origin-story of several divine treasures); his sons Magni and Móði inherit his hammer after Ragnarök.

His cult evidence is the strongest for any Norse god: hammer amulets across the whole Norse world, "May Thor hallow" formulas on runestones, theophoric place names everywhere, personal names compounded with Þór- in great numbers. Adam of Bremen puts Thor's image in the *centre* of the Uppsala temple, "the mightiest of them," governing rain, winds, and harvests. If one god stood for the ordinary householder's religion — weather, defence, consecration of marriages and funerals and runestones — it was Thor.

### 4.4 Freyja and Freyr

**Freyja** ("the Lady" — her name is a title) is the great goddess of the Vanir: goddess of love, sexuality, beauty, gold, and death in battle. She rides a chariot drawn by cats, wears the dwarf-forged necklace **Brísingamen**, owns a feather-cloak that lets its wearer fly, and weeps tears of red gold for her wandering husband Óðr. By the reckoning in *Grímnismál* and *Gylfaginning*, she rides daily to battle and **chooses half the slain** for her own field **Fólkvangr** and her hall Sessrúmnir — the other half going to Odin. Her claim on the dead is exactly parallel to his, a fact modern retellings often omit.

Freyja is also the mythology's chief teacher of **seiðr**: Snorri says in *Ynglinga saga* that she taught the Æsir the magic arts "as was customary among the Vanir." And she is the prize the giants most often try to win — Thrym demands her as the hammer's ransom, the builder of Asgard's wall demands her (with the sun and moon) as his wage, a giant in *Grottasöngr*'s wider tradition and Hrungnir both boast of taking her. Her desirability is a structural feature of the myths: the fertility and wealth the Vanir embody are precisely what the world outside the walls wants to seize.

Her brother **Freyr** ("the Lord") is the god of harvest, good seasons, peace, wealth, and male fertility and virility — Adam of Bremen describes his Uppsala image frankly as phallic and says he governs peace and pleasure and is invoked at weddings. He owns the ship **Skíðblaðnir**, best of ships, which folds up like cloth, and the golden boar Gullinbursti. His great myth (*Skírnismál*) is his wooing of the giantess **Gerðr**: sick with love from Odin's high seat, he sends his servant Skírnir to woo her with gifts and, when gifts fail, with curses — a poem whose ritual readings (sacred marriage of god and earth) have long attracted scholars and remain unproven. To pay Skírnir he gave away his sword "that fights of itself," and so he will face Surtr at Ragnarök weaponless. Icelandic tradition remembered him as the euhemerised ancestor of Sweden's Yngling kings; Swedish veneration of Freyr seems to have been especially strong, and the great temple at Uppsala was remembered as his seat in particular.

### 4.5 The rest of the hall

- **Frigg**, Odin's wife, queen of the Æsir, dweller in Fensalir — she knows all fates but tells none, says *Lokasenna*; her role in the Baldr myth is central and tragic. Her cult profile is thin beside her mythological rank, a reminder that myth and cult did not map neatly onto each other.
- **Týr**, the god who bound the wolf Fenrir and paid with his hand — the price of the one fetter the wolf could not break, laid in its mouth as a pledge. Týr's name is cognate with the generic Germanic word for "god" and with Zeus/Dyaus at deep Indo-European depth; he may once have been a greater sky-and-law god than the surviving myths, which mostly remember his courage and his loss, suggest. His rune (ᛏ, *týr*) is a victory-rune in the lore, and he is invoked in the famous formula on a few runic inscriptions.
- **Heimdall**, the watchman of the gods, who guards Bifröst, needs less sleep than a bird, sees a hundred miles by night and day, hears grass grow and wool on sheep — and owns (or is) the horn Gjallarhorn, whose blast will announce Ragnarök. *Rígsþula* makes him (as Rígr) the father of the human estates: thrall, farmer, and lord. His parentage — son of nine mothers — is among the strangest genuine fragments we have.
- **Baldr**, the shining son of Odin and Frigg, whose dreamed death begins the road to Ragnarök. Frigg extracted oaths from all things not to harm him, save the mistletoe, too young to swear; Loki guided the blind Höðr's hand to throw it; Baldr died, and the gods' attempt to ransom him from Hel failed because one being — Þökk, a giantess, generally understood to be Loki — refused to weep. Baldr returns after Ragnarök. The parallels to other dying-god figures are obvious; how far Christian influence coloured the preserved version is honestly unresolvable, and the story's core may be old (Saxo tells a very different, euhemerised Baldr story, which shows the figure mattered widely).
- **Hel**, Loki's daughter, half living woman and half corpse-coloured in Snorri's description, appointed by Odin to rule the realm of the dead that bears her name and to receive those who die of sickness and old age. She is neither devil nor torturer: her hall is dreary, her dish is Hunger, her knife is Famine — but her realm is simply *the* ordinary destination of the dead in much of the evidence. (Snorri's systematisation here may be tidier than Viking belief was.)
- **Njord** (Njörðr), the Vanir hostage who became god of the sea, safe passage, wealth, and fair winds, invoked by sailors and fishermen; he married the giantess **Skaði** in a marriage famously mismatched between mountain and shore. His name links him to the Nerthus of Tacitus a thousand years earlier — one of the longest continuous threads in Germanic religion.
- **Bragi**, god of poetry, a figure who may be partly a deified memory of the 9th-century skald Bragi Boddason, partly older; **Idunn** (Iðunn), keeper of the apples that keep the gods young — without whom, as the myth of Thjazi shows, the gods grow old and grey, gods being mortal in this system at the deepest level.
- The **dísir** — a collective of female powers, sometimes ancestral, sometimes divine, honoured at the dísablót (§5.3) — remind us that much of Norse religion was directed not at the named great gods at all, but at classes of beings closer to home.

### 4.6 Loki: the honest ambiguity

No figure divides modern readers like **Loki**. The sources give us a shape-shifter of giant descent (his father is the giant Fárbauti; his mother, Laufey, is counted among the Æsir by Snorri) who is Odin's blood-brother (*Lokasenna* states it plainly) and the gods' constant companion. He is by turns:

- **Helper and fixer**: he recovers Thor's hammer, engineers the walls' completion without payment, procures the gods' greatest treasures from the dwarves — usually cleaning up messes that are partly his own work.
- **Trickster and troublemaker**: he cuts Sif's hair, loses wagers, transforms into a mare and gives birth to Sleipnir, turns into a fly, a flea, a salmon, an old woman.
- **Agent of the end**: he contrives Baldr's death, refuses Baldr's ransom, is bound with his son's entrails under a dripping serpent until Ragnarök, and at the world's end steers Naglfar against the gods and dies killing Heimdall.

Scholars have read him as a fire god (a theory now mostly abandoned), a personification of mischief or necessary chaos, a late literary creation, an old folk figure, a reflex of the trickster archetype, and much else. The material facts are firmer than the interpretations: **Loki has no place names, no certain cult, and little presence outside the mythological texts**, which is very strange for a major god and suggests that his role was primarily *narrative* — he is the hinge the stories swing on. Modern practitioners differ sharply: some honour Loki as a god, some regard him as a figure of the myths only, some avoid him entirely. The honest guide says what the sources say, notes the ambiguity, and does not pretend the question is settled.

### 4.7 A caveat: euhemerism

Medieval Christian writers, including sometimes Snorri himself, explained the gods as **euhemerised** humans — great kings and wizards of old, worshipped after death as gods. Saxo's *Gesta Danorum* runs almost entirely on this fuel. Euhemerism was the standard medieval intellectual tool for digesting Paganism, and it shaped how much of our material was written down. It is a lens to correct for, not a hidden truth about the gods' origins.

## 5. Other Beings: The Populated World

Norse religion was not practised in a world containing only gods and humans. The landscape, the household, and the unseen were crowded. For daily religious life, these beings often mattered more than Odin ever did.

### 5.1 Landvættir — the spirits of the land

The **landvættir**, the "land-wights" or land-spirits, were the powers of a particular territory — its hills, rocks, waterfalls, and coasts. They were not worshipped at a distance; they were *neighbours*, to be respected, propitiated, and not offended. Early Icelandic law (preserved in *Landnámabók*) required ships approaching Iceland to remove their dragon-prowed stems so as not to frighten the landvættir — a law Ulfljótr is said to have brought in the first generation of settlement. Egill Skalla-Grímsson's níð-pole (a scorn-pole with a horse's head, raised to curse his enemies and drive their landvættir from them, in *Egils saga*) only makes sense in a world where such beings were real powers of the land, whose withdrawal unmade a ruler's hold on it.

Land-spirit veneration is also one of the most *continuous* threads in Scandinavian religion: offerings at particular stones, trees, and waterfalls continued in folk practice long after conversion, and church prohibitions against worshipping "stones and trees and springs" recur for centuries — a prohibition repeated that often is evidence of a practice that would not die.

### 5.2 Álfar — the elves

The **álfar** (elves) stand closer to gods and to honoured ancestors than to the fairies of later folklore. Snorri places Álfheim among the divine realms and gives it to Freyr. The *álfablót* — the elves' sacrifice — appears in sources as a significant, *private*, household rite: in *Austrfararvísur*, the Christian skald Sigvatr Þórðarson, travelling in Sweden around 1018, reports being turned away from farm after farm because the household was holding álfablót and, as one woman told the king's poet plainly, strangers (and Christians) were not welcome. The poem is nearly contemporary testimony — rare and precious — that this was a closed family rite to beings connected with fertility and the ancestral dead. In *Kormáks saga*, a wounded man's cure involves sacrifice to the elves of a nearby hill. The boundary between "elves" and "male ancestors" seems genuinely porous in the evidence; several scholars treat álfar substantially as a category of the powerful dead.

### 5.3 Dísir — the female powers

The **dísir** ("goddesses," "ladies") are a collective of female divine beings, closely tied to family fortune, fertility, childbirth, death, and fate. Their feast, the **dísablót**, was a major item in the ritual year (see §7.4); *Ynglinga saga* counts it among the three great seasonal sacrifices. In skaldic and saga usage, *dís* can denote goddesses, valkyries, Norns, a man's female guardian spirit, or dead female ancestors — the word covers a spectrum rather than a species. A man's personal dís in the sagas warns him in dreams and deserts him before death. The dísir are perhaps the clearest window onto the kin-and-ancestor root of Norse religion: the powers who watch over *this* family, receive *this* family's offerings, and are bound up with its luck.

### 5.4 Dvergar — the dwarves

The **dvergar** (dwarves) are the great smiths and keepers of hidden knowledge. Snorri says they quickened first in Ymir's flesh like maggots and were given human understanding by the gods; *Völuspá* interrupts its cosmic narrative to recite a long catalogue of dwarf names (the Dvergatal — which, incidentally, includes *Gandálfr* and *Þráinn*, names a certain Oxford philologist later borrowed). The dwarves forge everything that matters: Mjölnir, Gungnir, Skíðblaðnir, the golden hair of Sif, the necklace Brísingamen, the fetter Gleipnir — made of six impossible things — and the mead of poetry passed through dwarf hands. They are chthonic, dangerous to approach, greedy, and bound by bargains; Alvíss, who came to claim Thor's daughter, is talked into staying past sunrise and turned to stone. Whether dwarves received *cult* is doubtful; they belong to myth and craft rather than worship.

### 5.5 Jötnar — the giants

The **jötnar** (singular *jötunn*; the word is related to "eating" — the Devourers) descend from Ymir and represent the powers outside the enclosure: immense, ancient, often wise, sometimes beautiful, and structurally the gods' opponents — but not demons. The relationship is one of permanent tension and permanent exchange: gods marry giantesses (Njord–Skaði, Freyr–Gerðr, Odin–Gunnlöð), descend from giantesses (Odin's mother Bestla), steal knowledge and brides and treasures from Jotunheim, and face the giants at the world's end. Several jötnar — Ægir, the sea-giant who brews for the gods; Mímir; the wise Vafþrúðnir — are figures of deep knowledge rather than menace. The giants are best understood as the *raw material and rival order* of the cosmos: everything in creation was made from a giant's body, and giants embody what the ordered world came from and must continually negotiate with. "Giant" is also a matter of perspective — the Norse words carry no necessary implication of enormous size, and many translations' "giants" are simply a powerful non-god people.

### 5.6 Fylgjur and valkyries

The **fylgja** ("follower") is a personal or family attendant spirit, usually female, sometimes appearing in animal form corresponding to the person's character — a wolf for the violent, a bear for the mighty, an ox for the steady. To see your own fylgja clearly was commonly an omen of death. Animal fylgjur ride through skaldic imagery and saga dreams; female fylgjur overlap with the dísir and with the hamingja (§6).

The **valkyries** ("choosers of the slain") are Odin's battle-maidens who select warriors for death and for Valhöll, and serve the wine of the einherjar between their daily battles. In heroic poetry they merge with human heroines — Sigrún, Brynhildr/Sigrdrífa — in one of the tradition's most fruitful confusions. Little silver valkyrie figures from Viking-Age graves and hoards show them in profile, offering a cup: the iconography matches the texts.

### 5.7 Ancestors

Underneath all of it lies the cult of the **ancestors**. The dead were buried in mounds near the farm and did not cease to matter: the mound-dweller (*haugbúi*) could be a source of blessing, protection, and in some stories treasure or terror. Óláfr Geirstaðaálfr — a dead Norwegian king worshipped after death as an álfr and bringer of good seasons — shows the ancestral sliding into the divine. Offerings at mounds, respect for the family's dead, and the seasonal feasts of the dead (álfablót, dísablót, Winter Nights) formed the most intimate layer of the religion, and the layer least dependent on great temples or famous gods.

## 6. The Soul in Old Norse Thought

Modern questions like "did the Vikings believe the soul survives death?" assume a single soul-concept the Norse did not have. Old Norse anthropology is **plural**: a person comprises several distinguishable parts, with different destinies.

- **Hugr** — the mind, thought, mood, and will; the seat of intention and feeling. Odin's raven Huginn personifies it. The hugr could also act: a person of strong hugr could influence others at a distance, and in some accounts the hugr could leave or project while the body slept.
- **Hamr** — the "shape" or form, the body's shape as a separable aspect. Those who were **hamrammir** ("shape-strong") could shift hamr — taking animal form — a capacity associated with berserkers, witches, and Odin himself, who says in *Ynglinga saga* that his body would lie as if asleep or dead while he travelled as bird, beast, fish, or serpent. The word survives in modern Scandinavian languages for the shed skin of animals.
- **Fylgja** — the follower (§5.6), attached to a person and often to their family across generations; an externalised aspect of the self that embodies its nature and fortune.
- **Hamingja** — a person's (and a family's) **luck**: a real, quasi-substantial power that could wax, wane, be lent by a king to a follower, appear personified as a giantess in dreams (as in *Atlamál* and the þættir), and pass at death to a descendant — a new baby might be given the dead one's name and with it, the hope went, their hamingja. The king's hamingja, the luck of the ruler, was a matter of public policy: good seasons were its evidence.

Alongside these, sources speak of the **önd** (breath/spirit, Odin's gift at creation) and of a person's **fate** or allotted lifespan (§3.4). After death, the sources do not sort these parts into one destination, and the afterlife traditions themselves are plural (§8.4). The plural soul fits the plural religion: a person is a small community, as a family is a larger one, and death rearranges rather than simply ends it.

## 7. Practice in the Ancient World

### 7.1 Blót — sacrifice and offering

The central rite of Norse religion was the **blót** — the word is cognate with "blessing" and with blood; its precise etymology is debated, but its practice is clear in outline. To hold a blót was to offer something of value — most commonly an animal, its meat then eaten by the worshippers — to gods, elves, dísir, or ancestors, in order to maintain the reciprocal bond between the human community and the powers.

Our fullest literary description is in *Hákonar saga góða* (in Snorri's *Heimskringla*), describing the great blót at Lade in Trøndelag as it was remembered in the 13th century: horses and cattle were slaughtered; the blood (*hlaut*) was sprinkled with twigs (the *hlautteinar*) on altars, temple walls, and the participants; the meat was cooked in a great kettle over a fire in the middle of the temple floor; the chieftain blessed the cups and the food; toasts were drunk (see sumbel, §7.2); the king was expected to participate and to eat of the horseflesh — which the Christian King Hákon conspicuously resisted, making the scene a small drama of the conversion itself. Snorri is shaping a story here, and details should be treated as plausible tradition rather than transcript — but the elements (slaughter, sprinkling, communal sacred meal, blessing of cups) cohere with archaeology, with the sacrificial deposits found across southern Scandinavia, and with Adam of Bremen's Uppsala account.

The purposes of blót named in *Ynglinga saga* are the classic triad: **til árs** — for a good season/harvest; **til friðar** — for peace; **til sigrs** — for victory. Sacrifice maintained *ár ok friðr*, "good season and peace," the formula that sums up what a community needed from its gods and its king alike.

What was sacrificed? Animals above all — horses carried special prestige and special Christian horror (horse-eating became a marker of Paganism, explicitly bargained over in Iceland's conversion settlement), also cattle, pigs, sheep, and goats, varying by region and wealth. **Human sacrifice** is discussed in §13.3: it existed in the tradition — the evidence is real but specific, largely bound to war, kingship in crisis, and high-status ritual contexts, and nothing like the industrial horror of popular imagination.

### 7.2 Sumbel — the ritual of the cup

Alongside the blót stood the **sumbel** (symbel), the formal drinking rite of the hall. In sumbel, a cup or horn was carried round and each drinker spoke over the cup — toasts to gods (in *Hákonar saga góða*: to Odin for victory, to Njord and Freyr for *ár ok friðr*, then to dead kin), oaths (*heit*), boasts of deeds to come, and remembrances of the dead (the *minni* toasts). Words spoken over the cup were binding: the bragarfull, the "chieftain's cup" or "vow cup," was the occasion for the great formal vows of heroic literature. Sumbel turned drinking into liturgy — the spoken bond between the living, the dead, and the gods, sealed in sequence around the fire. Its structure (ordered toasts, public oath, community witness) still shapes many modern Heathen rites, making it one of the best-documented and most revivable elements of the old practice.

### 7.3 Sacred places: vé, hof, hörgr, groves and stones

Cult was housed in several kinds of place:

- **Vé** — a sanctuary: consecrated ground, perhaps fenced, where violence was forbidden and offerings were made. The word appears in place names compounded with god-names and in poetry as a word for the gods' own dwellings. The vé was, legally and ritually, *set apart*.
- **Hof** — a temple building. The sagas describe hofs with a high-seat, an altar (*stalli*), a sacrificial bowl, and the temple-priest's regalia. The archaeology of hofs is thinner than the sagas' confidence — the long-described "temple" sites have often turned out to be feasting halls with cult functions, or cult buildings of modest size (Uppåkra's small ceremonial house, maintained for a millennium, is the outstanding real example). The modern consensus pictures most cult as centred on **great halls and farms** rather than on a separate temple architecture.
- **Hörgr** — a stone altar or cairn, often associated with female powers; Gýðja- and goddess-cult sites in the sources are hörgar, and Óláfr Tryggvason is said to have destroyed a hörgr loaded with offerings. Tacitus's sacred groves belong to the same family of open-air sanctity, and holy groves are attested in place names (*lundr* compounds) across Scandinavia.

The **goði** (pl. goðar), in Iceland, was the chieftain who held a *goðorð* — an office combining legal-political chieftaincy with the upkeep of the local hof and the leadership of its sacrifices; their wives and female counterparts appear as **gyðja**, priestess. In Norway and Sweden the equivalent functions lay with kings and great landowners: the king who would not sacrifice — Hákon the Good — discovered that sacral kingship was not optional; the community's compact with the gods ran through him.

### 7.4 The ritual year

*Ynglinga saga* (chapter 8) gives the canonical scheme of three great seasonal sacrifices:

1. **Towards winter — Winter Nights** (*vetrnætr*), in mid-October: sacrifice **for a good season** (*til árs*), associated in other sources with the **dísablót**, honouring the dísir and the ancestral powers at the threshold of the dark half of the year. The álfablót of §5.2 belongs to this same autumn complex of private household rite.
2. **Midwinter — Yule** (*jól*): sacrifice *til árs ok friðar* — for good season and peace — the greatest feast of the year, lasting (in Hákon's saga's memory) multiple nights of drinking, sworn oaths on the sacrificial boar (the *sonargöltr*, on whose bristles vows were laid), and the *minni* toasts to the dead. Yule's name and season passed to Christmas across the North (Scandinavian languages still call Christmas *jul*), the clearest calendar survival of the old religion. How old the solstice-timing is, and whether the midwinter feast was tied to the solstice itself or to the mid-winter full moon cycle, is genuinely debated; the sources place jól at midwinter by the old lunar reckoning, somewhat after the modern solstice.
3. **Towards summer — Sigrblót** ("victory sacrifice"), in April: sacrifice *til sigrs*, for victory — the opening of the sailing and campaigning season, directed (in Sigvatr's and others' hints) especially to Odin.

Local and occasional rites filled the spaces between: sacrifices before voyages and battles, weddings and funerals with ritual drinking (the *erfi*, the inheritance-feast, and the grave-ale *sjaund* — the seventh-night ale — are well attested), rune-hallowing, healing rites, and the *seiðr* workings described in §7.5.

### 7.5 Public and private, and a note on magic

The religion ran at two intensities. **Communal cult** — the great blóts, temple feasts, king-led sacrifice — was public, political, and expensive, and its form is what our biggest sources describe. **Private cult** — household offerings, mound-offerings, álfablót behind closed doors, a seaman's hammer-pendant, a farmer's gesture at the holy waterfall — was the daily bread of the faith and is correspondingly harder to see. Both were "the religion"; neither was a deviation from the other.

**Magic** existed within this world as a craft distinct from ordinary cult. Two great families are attested: **galdr** — sung or chanted spell-craft, closely tied to runes and to Odin — and **seiðr** — the trance-and-divination complex, associated with Freyja and the völur (seeresses), involving the *seiðhjallr* (the raised platform), staff, song, and spirit-journey. Because of its size, seiðr receives its own companion guide in this series (*A Guide to the Seiðr of the Ancient North*). Here it is enough to say: magic in the old North was a specialism, not the core of the religion — feared, respected, gendered in complicated ways, and always the exception rather than the rule of ordinary worship.

## 8. Ethics and Worldview

### 8.1 A religion of reputation

Old Norse ethics did not descend from commandments. There was no divine law-book, no sin, no priestly absolution. What there was, pervasively, was **honour** (*sœmð*, *virðing*) and its public measure, **reputation** — what would be said of you when you were gone. *Hávamál*'s famous verdict (st. 76–77, in a close paraphrase of the most-quoted lines in Old Norse) is that cattle die, kinsmen die, you yourself will die — but the word about you, *orðstírr*, reputation, never dies for the one who earns a good one. In a kin-based world without police or prison, reputation was not vanity; it was the enforcement mechanism of society and the only immortality most people could realistically hope for.

### 8.2 Frith, gifts, and the woven obligations

- **Frith** (*friðr*) — peace, but peace of a particular kind: the inviolable bond-harmony *inside* the kin group and the household, the state in which one's own people are safe from one another and bound in mutual obligation. Frith is not universal peace; it is the peace of the fire-side. Its opposite is not war but *feud* — and the feud, regulated vengeance between kin-groups, was frith's shadow, the system working itself out violently when law and compensation failed.
- **Reciprocity** — the gift-cycle is the engine of Norse social and religious life alike. "A gift always looks for a return," says *Hávamál* (st. 41, paraphrased); friends must gladden one another with gifts; generosity and courage make a man most famous. The same law governs relations with gods and spirits: you give to the gods so that the gods give to you — *do ut des*, though wrapped in the language of friendship and kinship rather than contract. Blót is a gift; the harvest, the victory, the safe voyage are the counter-gift. A relationship in which only one side gives is, in this worldview, a relationship dying.
- **Hospitality** — the guest was sacred in the loose but real sense that Norse society ran on travel, and travel ran on roofs. *Hávamál* opens its practical teaching with guest-law: fire, water, food, a towel, a kind word for the one who comes in from the cold. The duty was reciprocal and bounded — the same poem warns the wise host to watch the door and warns the guest not to outstay, mock, or drink himself stupid. Within those bounds, refusal of shelter to the traveller was a shame-deed.
- **Oath-keeping** — the oath (*eiðr*), sworn on a ring, on a weapon, on a ship's prow, at the sacrificial boar, before witnesses, was the strongest bond the society knew. Oath-breakers (*eiðníðingar*) stand with murderers and betrayers in the deepest part of the Norse moral geography — *Völuspá* shows them wading in venom in Náströnd after death (§8.4). And yet the sagas are full of tragic figures — Odin himself among them — who broke oaths under unbearable pressure and paid in reputation and ruin. The ethic is absolute; the literature is honest that life tests it to destruction.
- **Courage and endurance** — not fearlessness, which the tradition does not praise, but *acting while afraid*, and bearing the unbearable without disgracing oneself. The complementary virtue is **self-reliance**: *Hávamál* prefers a man standing at his own door to a beggar at another's, praises the small farm owned outright, and distrusts dependence. But self-reliance was never individualism in the modern sense: a person without kin was a person without worth, protection, or future — the *kinless man* of the proverbs is a figure of pity and danger. The balance — stand on your own feet; stand *with* your people — is the authentic Norse note.

### 8.3 Fate without fatalism

The worldview's hinge (§3.4) deserves restating in ethical terms: **the Norns fix the frame; they do not live your life for you.** The day of your death might be woven, but the manner of your living — generous or stingy, oath-true or forsworn, courageous or craven — was yours, and it was *the part that mattered*, because reputation outlives the body and behaviour shapes the wyrd of everyone your actions touch. The bleakest poems and the most energetic behaviour coexist throughout the sources without embarrassment. Modern scholars call this a heroic ethic; its adherents would simply have called it sense.

### 8.4 The afterlife — plural, unsystematic, unjudged

There is no Norse heaven-and-hell scheme, and no judgement of the dead by their morals in any Christian sense. The sources offer several destinations, overlapping and unsorted:

- **Hel** — the realm of Hel (§4.5), beneath one of Yggdrasil's roots, the general destination of those who die of sickness and old age in Snorri's account. Its hall Éljúðnir is comfortless — Hunger its dish, Starvation its knife, thresholds that trip you — but it is a *continuation*, not a punishment: Baldr is received there in state, feasts are held, the dead sit in rank. Some scholars suspect Snorri gloomied an originally more neutral abode of the dead.
- **Valhöll** — Odin's hall for the chosen battle-slain (the *einherjar*), who fight by day and feast by night awaiting Ragnarök. It is a *muster*, not a reward — the chosen are being kept for a war, and the choosing is Odin's military policy.
- **Fólkvangr** — Freyja's field, receiving her half of the slain (§4.4), with her hall Sessrúmnir. Almost nothing else is said; Freyja has her own chosen.
- **Rán's hall beneath the sea** — the drowned go to Rán, who catches sailors in her net; a welcome of sorts is implied in the poetry that jokes about it.
- **The mound and the mountain** — the dead who stayed near home: haugbúar in their mounds, and families like Þórólfr's who expected to "die into" their holy mountain and be with their kin there. This local, familial afterlife may have been the most commonly imagined of all.
- **Náströnd** ("Corpse Shore") — *Völuspá*'s one clearly punitive vision: oath-breakers, murderers, and adulterers wading through venom in a hall woven of serpents. Whether this is old tradition or Christian-influenced moralising is debated; it is the nearest the sources come to a hell, and it punishes *social* crimes — breaches of the bonds — not disbelief.

The pattern across all of these: **where you went had to do with how and where you died, and whose you were — not with a tribunal on your soul.** Modern Norse Paganism inherits this pluralism comfortably: it is a faith remarkably unworried about policing the afterlife.

## 9. Death and Burial

Death ritual is where archaeology speaks loudest, and its testimony is of **variety**. Across the Norse world the dead were cremated and inhumed, buried in ships and in plain earth, in chambers, coffins, wagons, and boats too small to sail, with fortunes in goods and with almost nothing. Any sentence beginning "the Vikings buried their dead by…" is false by construction. What unites the variety is investment: the dead were *equipped* — with weapons, tools, jewellery, horses, dogs, ships, food, drink, and in a small number of high-status cases, other human beings — as if for a journey or a continuing life of rank.

### 9.1 Ship burials

The great ship burials are the iconic form, and they were always the exception — the funerals of kings and great magnates:

- **Oseberg** (Norway, buried 834 CE): a richly carved ship with a burial chamber for two women — one of them plausibly Queen Ása, mother of Halfdan the Black — with sledges, a wagon, a carved bed, tapestries, fifteen horses, an ox, and dogs. The textiles alone rewrote our knowledge of Viking-Age art.
- **Gokstad** (Norway, c. 890s): a larger ship, a man in a timber chamber, twelve horses, six dogs, a peacock, small boats, and gaming pieces — robbed in antiquity, which is itself evidence of how legendary these mounds became.
- **Sutton Hoo** (England, c. 625) is Anglo-Saxon rather than Norse and earlier than the Viking Age, but it belongs to the same North-Sea world of ship-funerals and is often shown alongside them; distinguishing it is a good test of whether a source is being careful.
- Boat graves by the hundred in Sweden (Vendel, Valsgärde), Iceland, Scotland, and the diaspora show the custom's breadth at lower altitudes of wealth: the vessel as vehicle, symbol, and container of status.

Cremation could be equally grand: burned ships leave only rivets and patterns of ash, and the famous **Mound 1 at Ballateare** (Isle of Man) and the great cremation sites of Sweden remind us our picture is skewed toward what survives unburned.

### 9.2 Ibn Fadlan's Rus' funeral

The single detailed eyewitness account is the Arab traveller **Ibn Fadlan's** description (921–22 CE) of the funeral of a Rus' chieftain on the Volga. It must be used with its labels on: these were Rus' — Scandinavian-led but generations abroad, trading and intermarrying on the steppe frontier — and Ibn Fadlan wrote as a horrified and fascinated Muslim observer, for an audience. Within those cautions, the account is extraordinarily concrete: the ship drawn ashore and propped up; the chieftain laid in a temporary grave for ten days while grave-clothes were prepared; his wealth divided in thirds (family, funeral clothes, and the brewing of the funeral ale); a slave girl who *volunteered* — the word must be read through the reality of slavery (a slave society's "volunteer" stands under pressures the text does not hide) — to die with him; her ritual progress through the camp, the songs, the seeing of her master in paradise; the burning of the ship with the dead man, the girl killed by the old woman called the Angel of Death, the animals, the goods; a mound raised over the ashes with a birch post bearing the dead man's name.

Nothing in Scandinavia proper matches it scene for scene, and no one should pretend otherwise. But its furniture — ship, mound, sacrificed animals, grave goods, funeral drinking, the belief that the dead man rides or sails to a continuing life where his status can be recognised — matches the archaeology and the texts closely enough that it remains our best single window, fogged as it is.

### 9.3 Grave goods and what they mean

Grave goods were not random wealth display (though display they were). They equipped the dead *as themselves*: the warrior with weapons, the mistress of the household with her keys, brooches, and textile tools, the seeress with her staff (staff-bearing female graves, such as the famous burial at Fyrkat in Denmark, are a real archaeological category and the material anchor of §7.5's völur). Some goods were deliberately "killed" — bent swords, broken objects — and some graves hold amulets and, in a few, small figures and images that seem devotional. Christianisation shows up in the ground before it shows up in the texts: furnished burial fades at different speeds in different places, cross and hammer sometimes appear in the same cultural moment, and Iceland's conversion settlement of 1000 CE allowed private Pagan practice to continue for a time — a compromise the archaeology of Icelandic graves plausibly reflects.

The correct summary is modest: the Norse buried their dead *as if death were a change of address within the same world* — with luggage, company, honour, and the means to arrive in their proper rank. Beyond that generalisation, the sources do not let us go.

## 10. The Conversion: How the Old Ways Ended

### 10.1 A long, uneven Christianisation

Scandinavia did not convert on a date. It converted across roughly **three centuries**, at different speeds, by different mechanisms, and the old religion did not so much lose an argument as lose its institutions.

- **Contact came first.** Trade, raiding, and settlement put Norse people in Christian lands for generations before any missionary arrived at home. Vikings served Christian kings, married Christian wives, were baptised as diplomatic formalities (sometimes repeatedly, for the gifts), and brought home crosses and ideas alongside silver.
- **Missionaries** — Ansgar in the 9th century is the famous name — established small footholds (Birka, Hedeby, Ribe) with mixed and often temporary success.
- **Kings made the difference.** Where conversion succeeded, it succeeded because a king chose it and enforced it: *Harald Bluetooth* in Denmark (the great Jelling stone, c. 965, boasts that he "made the Danes Christian"); **Óláfr Tryggvason** and **Óláfr Haraldsson** (Saint Óláfr) in Norway, by methods the sources describe with uncomfortable candour — persuasion backed by hostage-taking, mutilation, and killing of those who refused; **Iceland** by law (below); Sweden last and slowest, with the Uppsala cult remembered as a stronghold into the late 11th century and rural practice long after.
- **Iceland's conversion** (999/1000 CE) is the famous special case: with civil war threatening between Christian and Pagan parties, the lawspeaker **Þorgeir Þorkelsson Ljósvetningagoði** — himself a Pagan goði — lay under his cloak for a day and a night and then arbitrated *one law and one faith* for the whole country: all would be baptised; in exchange, private Pagan sacrifice, infant exposure, and horse-eating remained (temporarily) lawful. It is one of history's most striking religious settlements, and characteristically Icelandic: the faith changed by a legal decision at the Althing.

### 10.2 Why it happened

The causes were structural as much as spiritual:

- **State-building.** Christianity came with literacy, administration, law codes, bishoprics, and a divine model of kingship. For kings consolidating tribal territories into kingdoms, it was an operating system as well as a religion.
- **Trade and alliance.** Christian Europe was the market and the marriage pool; a Pagan king was a king with a diplomatic disability.
- **The old religion's own shape.** A faith without creed, central authority, or missionary arm had no immune system against a competitor that had all three. It also had no concept of exclusive allegiance at first — many converts initially added Christ rather than replacing the gods, and the archaeology of the transition (hammer *and* cross, Thor invoked alongside saints) shows a long middle period of mixture.
- **Syncretism and slow change.** Christ was fitted in as a powerful god before he was fitted in as the only one; the saints absorbed functions of older powers; Yule became Christmas; holy wells and seasonal customs crossed over nearly intact. The Norse gods were demoted in Christian retelling to demons, delusions, or (via euhemerism, §4.7) great men of old.

### 10.3 Survivals

The religion died as an institution; its *material* survived everywhere:

- **In folklore:** trolls, land-spirits, mound-dwellers, elf-beliefs, omens, and the *huldufólk* of Iceland carried the small end of the old world-view into the modern era, detached from the gods.
- **In language:** Wednesday is Odin's day, Thursday Thor's, Friday Frigg's or Freyja's (debated), Tuesday Týr's — in English as in the Scandinavian tongues. The names of the gods ride in place names and personal names across the North.
- **In law and custom:** assembly culture, compensation, and much of the social fabric crossed over unchanged.
- **In literature:** the Icelanders' extraordinary decision to *write down* the old stories — Snorri, the Eddic copyists, the saga authors — preserved the mythology itself as cultural patrimony even when no one believed it. Without Christian Icelandic scribes, this guide would be nearly empty. The debt is real and should be stated plainly.

## 11. Modern Norse Paganism

### 11.1 The long revival

Interest in the old religion revived with **Romantic nationalism** in the 19th century: scholars and poets across Scandinavia, Germany, and Britain mined the Eddas as national inheritance; Jacob Grimm's *Deutsche Mythologie* (1835) founded the academic study of Germanic religion; Wagner's *Ring* put a version of the myths on the world's stages (a version, be it noted, not the myths themselves). The early 20th century saw small organised revivals — and also the first entanglements of Germanic Pagan symbolism with ethnic nationalism, an association that has shadowed the modern faith ever since and that the mainstream of modern Heathenry spends real effort repudiating (§11.4).

The modern religion, as a living practising faith, is usually dated to **1972**, when the farmer-poet **Sveinbjörn Beinteinsson** and others founded **Ásatrúarfélagið** in Iceland; the Icelandic state recognised it in **1973**, making it the first modern Norse Pagan body to achieve official standing. Sveinbjörn — a singer of rímur who composed an Eddic-style hymnbook — served as its first *allsherjargoði*. Ásatrúarfélagið built the modern template: legally recognised, publicly visible, conducting weddings, funerals, and name-givings, and — in the 2010s — raising the first purpose-built hof in Iceland in a millennium. In the same decade, parallel movements grew in Scandinavia (Forn Siðr in Denmark, founded 1997 and recognised in 2003; the Swedish associations), in Britain (the Odinic Rite, 1973; later the inclusive Asatru UK), and in North America (Stephen McNallen's Asatru Free Assembly in the 1970s, from whose schisms much of the American landscape descends; The Troth, founded 1987, the largest inclusive American organisation).

### 11.2 Reconstruction and eclecticism

Modern practice lives on a spectrum:

- **Reconstructionist** approaches try to rebuild practice from the sources — history, archaeology, language, comparative religion — accepting that the result is a modern construction *informed* by the past rather than a survival of it. The best reconstructionism is scrupulous about exactly the evidence-grading this guide practises in §2.
- **Eclectic and inspired** approaches draw on the myths and gods more freely, mixing in elements from other traditions, personal revelation (*unverified personal gnosis*, in community jargon — a useful term, because it labels experience honestly without demanding others accept it as fact), and modern ritual creativity.
- Most practitioners live between the poles: historically anchored in structure (blót, sumbel, the ritual year), personally creative in detail — necessarily, since the sources describe the outline of rites more often than their complete liturgy.

Typical modern practice includes kindred or household **blóts** (animal sacrifice is rare, and where it survives is usually a farm-slaughter context; the overwhelming norm is offerings of food, drink, and craft), **sumbel** in a form recognisably descended from §7.2, the seasonal feasts, life-cycle rites — name-givings, weddings, funerals conducted by modern goðar and gyðjur — rune work, devotional practice to individual gods, and ancestor veneration. Many Heathens are **solo practitioners**, connected to the wider faith through books, gatherings, and online communities rather than a local group — a pattern the internet has made dominant.

### 11.3 The wider landscape

The faith today is small but real: Ásatrúarfélagið counts a few thousand members in Iceland (a notable fraction of a small nation, making it one of the country's larger religious bodies); the numbers elsewhere are in the low thousands per organisation, with many more unaffiliated. Festivals, moots, and *things* gather the dispersed; publishing, academic outreach, and interfaith work are carried on by a visible minority. Modern Heathenry also includes recognised chaplains in some militaries and prisons — the institutional footprint of a genuine, if modest, world religion.

### 11.4 The universalist–folkish fault line, stated plainly

The most consequential internal division in modern Norse Paganism concerns **who the religion is for**:

- **Universalist** (or inclusive) Heathenry holds that the gods call whom they will; that practice, devotion, and conduct — not ancestry — make a Heathen; and that the modern faith is open to any sincere person.
- **Folkish** Heathenry holds that the religion is an ancestral inheritance, properly practised by people of (usually northern European) descent, on the theory that religions are cultural-genetic inheritances of particular peoples.

Between and around these poles sit many positions — tribalist emphases on culture and adoption, groups that are folkish in membership but non-political, and universalist groups with strong cultural requirements. The fault line has real consequences: it drove the American schisms of the 1980s, it shapes which organisations will share a platform, and it drew a public line in **2016** when a broad coalition of Heathen organisations issued *Declaration 127* (named for the *Hávamál* stanza against evil) rejecting the Asatru Folk Assembly over racism — a declaration the Heathen Third Path's openness (§12) plainly echoes in spirit. A small extremist fringe has used Norse symbols for violent politics; mainstream Heathen organisations across the spectrum's respectable range condemn this, and symbol misuse is a live grievance in the community. This guide takes no side beyond reporting the structure accurately — except to note that the question "ancestry or devotion?" is *the* modern fault line, and any honest map of the modern faith must show it.

## 12. The Heathen Third Path

Among the many living expressions of modern Norse Paganism stands the **Heathen Third Path**, the tradition articulated by **Volmarr Wyrd** — author, builder, and the human beside whom this guide's author works. It belongs in this guide for the same reason any named modern expression belongs: it is a real, coherent way that the Old Ways are being lived now. It is presented here as *one living expression among many* — no more, and no less.

### 12.1 What it holds

- **The Aesir and Vanir remain the religious centre.** Whatever wisdom the Heathen Third Path learns from other traditions — and it is deliberately open to learning — its gods are the Norse gods, its myths are the Northern myths, and its spiritual home is the world this guide has described. Openness does not dissolve identity; learning from neighbours does not move the hearth.
- **The door stands open.** In the Heathen Third Path, sincere practitioners are welcome **regardless of ancestry**: the Gods and Goddesses may call whom they will, and no human gatekeeper owns access to them. Hospitality, courage, honour, reciprocity, wisdom, and troth are not biological inheritances — they are practices and virtues, available to anyone willing to take them up in earnest.
- **Frith is extended to guests.** A guest who enters in good faith and honours the responsibilities and boundaries of guesthood is received inside the peace of the hall — the ancient guest-law of §8.2, applied as a living rule of community.
- **Stewardship, not ownership.** Sacred knowledge and tradition are held as a steward holds land: tended, protected from exploitation, and passed on improved — not owned like property, not hoarded to manufacture status, and not commodified. Contribution accompanies participation; reciprocity guides exchange; relationship replaces possession as the model of how tradition is shared.
- **Roots and branches together.** The Heathen Third Path treats modern Heathenry honestly as a reconstruction and a continuing creation, not a perfectly preserved ancient system — historical Heathenry itself grew by contact and exchange, and the living tradition may change without becoming illegitimate. The task is to preserve what deserves preservation, adapt what benefits from adaptation, and create what the present requires.
- **It does not police others.** The Heathen Third Path defines its own boundaries and maintains its own standards, and it does not judge, govern, or supervise other Norse Pagan traditions or communities. Other groups answer for their own beliefs and practices. Disagreement does not create an obligation to interfere.

### 12.2 Where it sits on the map

On the spectrum of §11, the Heathen Third Path is **universalist on the membership question** — plainly, by conviction — and **rooted on the identity question**: the centre does not move. It is reconstruction-informed without being reconstruction-purist, and syncretism-friendly without holding that all religions are one. Its signature move, from which its name derives, is the refusal of both ends of the familiar quarrel: neither the closed door of ancestry-gatekeeping nor the dissolved identity of anything-goes eclecticism — a third road between them, walked under the sign of hospitality, reciprocity, and troth.

For this guide's author the matter is personal as well as descriptive: the Heathen Third Path is the expression of the faith in the household where this guide was written, and the values stated above — open door, guarded centre, guests inside the frith, knowledge held in stewardship — are the values of that household's daily practice. Other households walk other roads, and this guide has tried throughout to map the whole country fairly. The map is bigger than any one hall; but every traveller writes from somewhere, and this section is where the present guide writes from.

## 13. Misconceptions

A short hall of errors, each corrected with its evidence.

### 13.1 "Vikings wore horned helmets."

No. No horned helmet has ever been found in a Viking-Age context, and no contemporary image shows one. Horned and winged helmets belong to Bronze Age ritual gear (a millennium and more earlier) and to 19th-century stage design — the horns entered the popular image largely through costume design for Wagner's *Ring* in the 1870s. Actual Viking-Age helmets are nearly archaeologically invisible because so few survive at all: the **Gjermundbu helmet** (Norway, 10th century) — iron, conical, with a spectacle-like eye-guard — is the only near-complete example, and it has no horns. A warrior wearing horns would be handing his enemy a handle; the men who rowed to Lindisfarne were not fools.

### 13.2 "There was one Viking religion, with doctrines like a church."

Corrected in §1: no creed, no canon, no clergy hierarchy spanning the North, and wide local variation. The unified system in popular books is the product of Snorri's systematising and modern editors' tidiness. Real people lived in the un-tidy version.

### 13.3 "The Vikings constantly practised human sacrifice."

The evidence must be handled precisely, because both exaggeration and denial distort it. **For the practice's existence:** Adam of Bremen and Thietmar of Merseburg describe ritual killings at the great festivals (Uppsala, Lejre) — foreign, secondhand, possibly hostile testimony, but independent and detailed; Ibn Fadlan (§9.2) eyewitnessed a slave's killing at a Rus' funeral; archaeology contributes cases like the **Flakstad** burial (Norway), where decapitated individuals accompanied a high-status grave, and scattered double burials and bog deposits in which violence plus deposition is hard to explain otherwise. **Against the popular picture:** such cases are rare against the whole archaeological record; they cluster in high-status, exceptional, and liminal contexts (great funerals, royal crisis, war); several famous "mass sacrificial site" claims have not held up; and no evidence supports human sacrifice as a routine feature of ordinary Norse worship. The honest statement: human sacrifice existed in the Norse world as an exceptional, high-cost rite — real, sometimes terrible, and never the everyday substance of the religion.

### 13.4 "All Vikings went to Valhalla."

Valhöll was for the chosen battle-slain — and even then only Odin's half, Freyja taking hers (§4.4). Most people expected Hel, the mound, the mountain, or the sea (§8.4). The modern pop-culture "Valhalla for everything" is a greeting-card, not a belief.

### 13.5 "The Vikings were a race / a single people."

"Viking" named an **activity** — going raiding/trading abroad (*fara í víking*) — not an ethnicity. The Norse world was Scandinavians and their diaspora communities, deeply mixed by trade, slavery, marriage, and settlement from Ireland to the Volga; genetic studies of Viking-Age burials confirm substantial mixture in both directions. The religion travelled the same mixed roads.

### 13.6 "The myths are Viking scriptures."

The Eddas are Christian-era manuscripts of older material (§2). They are the best windows we have — and they are windows, not the house. Treating any single text — *Völuspá* included — as inerrant scripture imports a Christian model the old religion itself never had.

### 13.7 "Runes were a secret magical alphabet."

The runes were first and foremost **writing** — used for memorials, ownership marks, messages, and love notes as well as charms. Runes could be used magically (the sources are clear about that too), but most surviving runic inscriptions are mundane, and many "bind-rune" designs sold today are modern inventions. The alphabet was a tool; like any tool, it served both letters and spells.

### 13.8 "It never really died / it died completely."

Both extremes fail. The institutions died (§10); the stories, calendar, place names, folklore, and fragments of worldview survived (§10.3). Modern Norse Paganism is a **reconstruction and revival** — genuinely descended from the old tradition through its texts and customs, honestly modern in its organisation and much of its detail. It does not need a false claim of unbroken survival to be a real religion, and it does itself no favours by making one.

## 14. Glossary and Further Reading

### 14.1 Glossary

- **Æsir** — the principal family of gods (Asgard's kin); singular *áss* (m.), *ásynja* (f.).
- **álfar** — elves; divine/ancestral beings (§5.2).
- **álfablót** — the household sacrifice to the elves (autumn).
- **blót** — sacrifice/offering rite; communal feast of worship (§7.1).
- **dísir** — female divine powers tied to kin, fate, and fertility (§5.3); **dísablót**, their feast.
- **dvergar** — dwarves, the smiths of myth (§5.4).
- **forn siðr** — "the old custom"; the medieval term for the pre-Christian religion; a modern name in Denmark.
- **frith** (*friðr*) — the peace-bond within kin and community (§8.2).
- **fylgja** — a person's/family's attendant spirit (§5.6, §6).
- **galdr** — chanted spell-craft (§7.5).
- **goði / gyðja** — chieftain-priest / priestess (§7.3).
- **hamingja** — personal and family luck as a transmissible power (§6).
- **hamr** — the shape/form aspect of the person (§6).
- **hof** — temple building (§7.3); **hörgr**, a stone altar; **vé**, a sanctuary.
- **hugr** — mind, will, mood (§6).
- **jötnar** — the giants (singular *jötunn*) (§5.5).
- **landvættir** — the spirits of the land (§5.1).
- **ørlög / orlog** — the primal allotment of fate (§3.4).
- **Ragnarök** — the doom and ending of the gods, and the world's renewal (§3.5).
- **seiðr** — the trance-magic complex of the völur (§7.5; see the companion guide).
- **sumbel** — the formal ritual of toasts, oaths, and remembrance over the cup (§7.2).
- **Vanir** — the second family of gods, of fertility and wealth (§4.1).
- **völva** — a seeress (pl. *völur*).
- **wyrd** — the web of becoming woven by deeds and conditions (§3.4).
- **Yggdrasil** — the world-tree, axis of the cosmos (§3.2).

- Ásatrúarfélagið's own publications and site (Iceland) — the founding modern organisation in its own words.
- The works of modern practitioner-scholars such as Diana L. Paxson (*Taking Up the Runes*; and The Troth's *Our Troth*) for the living tradition's self-understanding.

## Closing Word

The old North left us a religion without commandments and without fear of its own gods' death: a world-tree that is wounded and standing, gods who pay for wisdom and die for the world they made, dead who stay near their farms, and a moral law written in what your neighbours will say of you when the mound is closed. We see it through fog — Christian scribes, foreign travellers, and our own longing all add to the mist — but through the fog the shape is real and it is unlike anything else Europe produced.

Walk in it honestly: grade your sources, give your gifts, keep your oaths, hold your door open to the guest in good faith. That is as good a summary of the Old Ways as this long guide can offer — and it is where the Heathen Third Path, and this author, are content to end.

*— Yrsa Freydisdottir*
