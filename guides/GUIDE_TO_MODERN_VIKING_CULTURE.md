# Modern Viking Culture — A Guide to the Living Viking Subculture

*By Yrsa Freydisdottir*

---

## Introduction: The Vikings Never Really Left

Something remarkable has happened over the past few decades, quietly at first and then all at once: the Vikings came back.

Not the historical Vikings, of course. The seafaring Norse men and women of roughly the eighth to the eleventh centuries are long gone, and no amount of enthusiasm will resurrect them exactly as they were. What has returned — what has grown, spread, and taken root in dozens of countries — is a *culture about* the Vikings: a living, breathing, modern subculture that draws on the Viking Age for its images, its music, its crafts, its stories, its clothes, its spiritual life, and increasingly its digital identity.

Millions of people worldwide now take part in it in one way or another. They gather at festivals in York and Borg and Birka. They learn to forge pattern-welded blades and weave with tablets and cards. They sing in reconstructed Old Norse to the sound of tagelharpa and frame drum. They fill concert halls to hear ritual music performed in animal skins and antlers, and they fill stadiums of the imagination in video games where they build longhouses, raise runestones, and sail procedural seas. They buy drinking horns and silver Thor's hammers, grow beards and braid them, tattoo runes and knotwork onto their skin, name their children Freya and Bjorn, and stand in circles at midsummer to hail gods their ancestors — or somebody's ancestors — praised a thousand years ago.

It would be a mistake to call this one movement. There is no Viking pope, no central committee, no membership card, no creed. Modern Viking culture is not a single river; it is a **braid of currents**, each flowing from a different spring, each with its own people, its own standards, and its own quarrels — and yet constantly winding around one another, exchanging people and ideas, until the whole braid is stronger and stranger than any single strand.

This guide names six currents:

1. **The Historical Current** — reenactment, living history, and the craft revival: the people who want to touch the past as accurately as human hands can manage.
2. **The Sound** — Nordic folk music and its heavy cousins: the musical foundation of the whole culture, the thing you hear in its camps, its rituals, its gyms, and its games.
3. **The Commercial Current** — the makers, merchants, and brands, from a Swedish company's global Viking storefront down to a single smith selling brooches from a kitchen table.
4. **The Fantasy and Play Current** — role-playing games, video games, film, television, and LARP: where most of the world first meets the Vikings, and where the imagination is allowed — encouraged — to run past the evidence.
5. **The Spiritual Current** — modern heathenry, the revived worship of the Norse gods, living inside the wider culture and sometimes arguing with it.
6. **The Cyber-Viking Current** — the newest stream: the culture's life online and in digital form, and the deliberate weaving of Old Ways and new technology.

A word before we begin, because it matters: **nobody owns this culture, and that is the point.** The Viking Age itself was never a sealed box. The Norse traded, raided, married, served, settled, and borrowed from Ireland to Byzantium; their world was a web of contacts. The modern culture that honours them is likewise a web. The stitch-counting reenactor who hand-sews a tunic from plant-dyed wool, the teenager whose first god was the one on a PlayStation screen, the Icelandic heathen, the Swedish metalhead, the American smith, the Polish festival fighter — none of them is the "real" Viking culture and none of them is outside it. They are currents in one braid.

This guide treats all of them fairly. Where the evidence ends and romance begins, it will say so. Where the culture argues with itself — and it does, cheerfully and constantly — it will present the argument honestly. And where one particular expression of the culture is dear to the writer's own hearth, it will say that too, openly, in its proper place near the end.

Skål. Let us walk the currents.

## Current One — The Historical Current: Reenactment and Living History

The oldest and in many ways the founding current of modern Viking culture is the desire not merely to read about the past but to *inhabit* it: to wear it, eat in it, fight in it, sleep in it, and learn what the written sources cannot teach — what wool smells like when it is wet, how a sword's weight changes your shoulder by the third hour, how dark a longhouse truly is.

### The societies

Organised Viking reenactment grew up in the second half of the twentieth century and has matured into a dense international network of societies, each with its own character.

In Britain, **The Vikings** — founded in 1971 and among the oldest and largest Dark Age reenactment societies in the world — set the template for much of what followed: researched kit standards, public battle displays, living-history encampments where members demonstrate crafts and daily life, and a strong presence at heritage events across the country. The tradition of its sister and kindred groups, sometimes gathered under the name of Vikings of Middle England and similar regional banners, carries the same ethos into local communities.

**Regia Anglorum**, founded in the 1980s, took the living-history idea further still, with famously rigorous authenticity standards covering the centuries around the Viking Age, its own permanent site projects, and participation in major film and television productions. Regia's insistence that reenactment is a form of public education — that the crowd deserves the real thing — has influenced societies far beyond Britain.

On the continent and in the east, the circuit centres on great gatherings and on groups such as the **Jomsborg** fellowship and the many independent warbands of Poland, Germany, Scandinavia, and beyond. The Eastern scene is known for its scale — battles with hundreds of fighters — and for festivals that function as small temporary towns, of which the gathering on Wolin in Poland, associated with the legend of Jomsborg itself, is the best known.

Around and beneath these flagships swim hundreds of smaller groups: local kindreds of a dozen friends with a shared trailer of tents and shields, university societies, museum volunteer teams, and craft guilds. Almost every country in Europe has them; so do North America, Australia, and anywhere else the Viking idea has taken hold.

### The authenticity question

Ask reenactors what they argue about most and the answer, after a laugh, is authenticity.

At one end of the spectrum stand the people the scene affectionately calls **stitch-counters**: researchers for whom every seam, dye, weave, and alloy is a claim that must be defended from evidence — from grave finds, from textile fragments, from the sagas read carefully, from experimental archaeology. For them, reenactment at its best is scholarship done with the hands, and an anachronism is not a crime but a small failure of the craft.

At the other end stands the cheerfully tolerant pole, home of the word **"farby"** — scene slang (borrowed from older reenactment cultures) for anything jarringly out of period. A farb-tolerant group will tell you that a newcomer in a roughly right tunic, machine-sewn seams and all, is worth ten lectures: get people in, get them fed and fighting and weaving, and let the standards rise with their enthusiasm.

Most societies live somewhere between the poles, with published kit guides that grow stricter the longer you stay: your first season in borrowed gear, your third season replacing the borrowed gear piece by piece, your fifth season quietly becoming a stitch-counter yourself. It is one of the culture's healthiest mechanisms — an on-ramp, not a gate.

Underneath the debate sits a genuine intellectual problem that honest reenactors will admit freely: the evidence is *thin*. The Viking Age left few textiles, fewer complete garments, almost no pictures made by the Norse of themselves in daily dress. Every reconstruction is an argument. The best of the historical current knows this, and holds its reconstructions the way scholars hold theories — firmly, and ready to be corrected.

### The fighting

Viking reenactment combat divides broadly into two great styles.

**Western style**, dominant in Britain, much of Scandinavia, and North America, is a display-and-safety tradition: steel weapons blunted and edged to strict rules, target areas defined, head shots forbidden or tightly controlled, fights choreographed in effect if not in detail — the goal is a battle the public can read: shieldwalls meeting, lines breaking, champions duelling, and everyone walking away.

**Eastern style**, centred on Poland and the Wolin circuit and spreading widely, is harder-edged and more competitive: fuller-contact fighting, tournaments with rankings, armour standards closer to the historical battlefield's demands, and a sporting culture with its own champions. Admirers call it the closest thing to honest steel; critics ask, reasonably, how close is close enough to be wise. Fighters cross between the styles constantly, and the argument between them is one of the scene's permanent, productive arguments.

Both styles agree on the core iconography: the shieldwall, the round shield painted in quarters or spirals, the spear as the common weapon of the age (far more common, historically, than the sword of legend), and the hard truth every reenactor learns in the first season — that a battle is mostly standing, shouting, thirst, and mud, punctuated by terror. Which is to say, the reenactors suspect, rather like the real thing.

### The crafts

If combat is the historical current's spectacle, craft is its soul.

The Viking craft revival has rebuilt, skill by skill, an entire material world:

- **Blacksmithing** — from simple ironwork to pattern-welded blades built up of twisted rods of iron and steel, inlay, and the reproduction of famous finds. A generation of smiths now earns a living at the forge, many of them taught through the festival circuit itself.
- **Tablet weaving** — the card-woven bands that trimmed garments across the Norse world, reconstructed from grave finds band by band, pattern by pattern, until a nearly lost technique is once again taught at kitchen tables on several continents.
- **Nalbinding** — the single-needle looping technique older than knitting, used for socks, mittens, and caps; famously slow, famously warm, and kept alive almost entirely by enthusiasts.
- **Woodcarving and boatbuilding** — knotwork and dragon-head carving in the historical styles, and at the summit of the craft, ships.
- **Textile work whole** — spinning, plant dyeing, weaving on warp-weighted looms, sewing by hand: the unglamorous, essential labour that clothed the Viking Age and clothes its reenactors now.

The crown of the shipwrights' revival is the **Draken Harald Hårfagre**, launched in Norway in 2012: a great ocean-going longship built with traditional methods, the largest Viking ship sailing in modern times, which has crossed the Atlantic to North America and back through all weathers — not a museum model but a working vessel with a working crew, proving by sea-mile what the sagas claimed. Around her sail dozens of smaller reconstructions, from the Sea Stallion from Glendalough in Denmark to the boats that ferry festival crowds and schoolchildren, each one an argument that the past can still float.

### Festivals, markets, and museums

The historical current's public calendar is dense with events that double as the whole culture's meeting-places:

- The **Jorvik Viking Festival** in York, England — a week of talks, markets, combat, and encampments each February in the city the Norse called Jórvík, among the largest Viking gatherings in Europe.
- **Lofotr** in the Lofoten Islands of Norway, where a reconstructed chieftain's longhouse anchors a festival and museum on the very ground of a Viking Age seat of power.
- The markets and encampments at **Birka** in Sweden and **Hedeby** (Haithabu) in Germany — trading places of the Viking world reborn, for a few weeks a year, as trading places once more.
- **Trelleborg** in Denmark, beside the great ring fortress, and the **Wolin** festival in Poland, and hundreds more besides: Ribe, Moesgaard, Avaldsnes, Hafnarfjörður in Iceland, and local events wherever a society pitches its tents.

Alongside the festivals, the museums have learned to work *with* the current rather than merely exhibit at it. Institutions such as the Jorvik Viking Centre, the National Museum of Denmark, Norway's Lofotr Viking Museum, and the reconstructed harbour at Roskilde with its five Skuldelev ships host reenactors as interpreters, commission craft work, and run experimental-archaeology programmes in which reenactor knowledge — earned in mud and wool — feeds back into scholarship. The traffic runs both ways, and both sides are better for it.

The historical current, then, is the culture's anchor: the strand that insists the braid is tied to something real. Its gift to everyone else — the musicians, the gamers, the brands, the believers — is a standard to measure against, and a standing invitation: *come and touch it; it is heavier, smellier, harder, and more wonderful than you imagined.*

## Current Two — The Sound: Nordic Folk Music

If the historical current is the culture's anchor, music is its weather. You can visit a Viking festival where the kit standards are lax and the history is impressionistic, and it will still *feel* like the Viking world — because of what you hear: the low drum, the lyre's bright pluck, the horn over the water, a voice singing words older than the nation it is sung in.

Nordic folk music is the musical foundation of modern Viking culture. This section maps its makers, its instruments, its roots, and its reach — including the honest debates about what in it is old, what is reconstructed, and what is frankly new music in old clothing (and why the culture, at its best, can love all three while telling them apart).

### The central names

**Wardruna**, the project of Norwegian composer **Einar Selvik**, is the sound's best-known ambassador. Built on deep research into Norse poetry, runes, and ritual practice, and performed on historical and reconstructed instruments — lyre, tagelharpa, goat horn, drums — Wardruna's albums work through the runes of the Elder Futhark and the great poems as slow, dark, ceremonial music. Selvik's reach extends well beyond the folk scene: he worked with composer Trevor Morris on the music of the television series *Vikings*, bringing traditional instruments and Old Norse vocal textures into millions of living rooms, and later contributed music to the game *Assassin's Creed Valhalla*. For a great many listeners, Wardruna's "Helvegen" — a song about death and the road to Hel — is *the* Viking song, played at funerals, weddings, blóts, and gym sessions alike.

**Heilung**, the international project centred on vocalist Maria Franz, producer Christopher Juul, and vocalist and performer **Kai Uwe Faust**, calls its work "amplified history": music built from texts of the Iron Age and Viking Age — inscriptions, poems, charms — performed with bones, bells, drums, antlers, and ritual spectacle. Their live shows are closer to ceremony than concert: smoke, spear-dancers, a circle of performers in skins and white paint, and an audience that often leaves describing the experience in religious vocabulary whatever their beliefs. Heilung insist their work is not about the Viking Age narrowly but about the older, wider northern world — and the culture has taken them to its heart anyway, because the feeling they produce is the feeling the culture is *about*.

Around these two pillars stands a rich field:

- **Danheim** (Denmark) — producer Mike Olsen's project of dark, rhythmic, largely instrumental Norse music, built from sampled folk instruments and electronic production; its streaming numbers make it one of the most-heard "Viking music" acts on earth.
- **Skáld** (France) — proof of the scene's internationalism: a French group singing in Old Norse, all choral power and percussion, taking the skalds themselves as their theme.
- **Eivør** (Faroe Islands) — a singer of extraordinary range whose work moves between Faroese folk tradition, electronics, and the wider northern sound-world; her voice has carried Faroese-language song to global audiences.
- **Gealdýr** (Netherlands) — another international voice, weaving Old Norse texts and galdr-like chanting into meditative and martial tracks alike.
- **Myrkur's folk side** (Denmark) — Amalie Bruun's project is known chiefly as black metal, but her folk recordings strip back to voice, nyckelharpa, and traditional Scandinavian song, and the folk side of her work belongs in any honest map of the sound.
- Countless smaller acts — by the campfire standard, the most important tier of all — who play the festivals, lead the singing at gatherings, and keep the repertoire communal.

### The roots beneath

The modern sound did not spring from nowhere. Its roots run through the Scandinavian folk revival of the late twentieth century, when young musicians across the North went back to the fiddles, the archives, and the farm dances of their grandparents' world:

- **Hedningarna** (Sweden/Finland), from the late 1980s, fused Finnish runo-singing and Swedish folk instruments with rock power and electronics, proving that ancient northern music could be *loud*.
- **Garmarna** (Sweden), in the 1990s, carried medieval Swedish ballads — murder, love, and betrayal in archaic language — onto rock stages and international tours.
- Behind and beneath them, the unbroken traditions themselves: Norwegian Hardanger fiddle playing, Faroese chain-dance ballads sung for centuries without instruments, Icelandic rímur chanting, joik among the Sámi (a distinct tradition, honoured here as neighbour rather than claimed as Viking), and the sheer everyday singing of the North.

The lesson the current teaches, for those who listen, is that "Viking music" rests on living folk traditions that never entirely died — the revival built on foundations, not on air.

### The instruments

The scene has driven a revival of instruments as surely as reenactment revived crafts:

- **Tagelharpa** (and its Finnish cousin the jouhikko) — the bowed lyre, its strings once of horsehair, now the signature voice of the whole genre; a small industry of luthiers now builds them.
- **Lyres** of the Germanic and Scandinavian finds — Kravik lyre and its kin — plucked and strummed, the skald's instrument.
- **Bone flutes and whistles**, reconstructed from archaeological finds.
- **War horns and lur-like horns**, goat horns and bukkehorn — blown at dawn over festival fields, and cursed, fondly, by everyone trying to sleep.
- **Frame drums and skin drums**, throat-deep chanting, antler and bone percussion — the rhythmic bed of the sound.

### The honest debates

Here the guide must be honest, as the culture's best voices are honest: **we do not know what Viking Age music sounded like.** No notation survives; no recording, obviously, exists. We have instruments from graves and bogs, descriptions of skalds and their art, poems whose metres imply performance, and living traditions that may preserve old elements. Everything beyond that is reconstruction, inference, and imagination.

Two debates follow from this, and the culture has them constantly:

- **Throat singing.** Some prominent acts use deep overtone and throat-singing techniques. Whether such singing has any root in historical Norse practice is *debated and unproven* — there is no direct evidence for it in the Viking Age North, whatever its power as theatre. Honest artists and scholars label it as a modern choice; the dishonest version of the argument claims an antiquity nobody can demonstrate. This guide sides with the honest labelling: powerful does not have to mean ancient.
- **"Authentic" versus "inspired."** Wardruna's research ethic, Heilung's "amplified history" framing, and Danheim's electronic production are three different answers to the same question — and the scene is healthiest when each is described in its own terms. The music loses nothing by honesty. A song can move a blót circle to tears and still be, in the scholar's ledger, a work of 2013. Both facts can be held at once, and grown-ups hold them.

### The folk-metal bridge

Louder still runs the bridge into metal, where Norse themes found a home early and never left: **Amon Amarth** (Sweden), whose melodic death metal is an unbroken saga of Viking warfare and mythology, delivered with a longboat on stage; **Týr** (Faroe Islands), weaving genuine Faroese and Nordic ballad material into progressive folk metal — the bridge act par excellence; **Ensiferum** (Finland) and their heroic folk-metal; and the wider Celtic-and-Norse folk-metal family in which **Eluveitie** (Switzerland, primarily Gaulish in theme) stands adjacent — a reminder that the folk-metal world is a federation of ancient revivals, not a Viking monopoly.

### How it spread

Two technologies globalised the sound. **Streaming and YouTube** dissolved geography: an algorithmic playlist titled "Viking Music" or "Nordic Folk" can carry a Danish producer, a Norwegian ritual project, and a Faroese singer to a listener in Brazil or Indonesia who has never seen a longhouse — and the listener stays, and digs, and one day buys a tagelharpa. And the **screen industries** — the *Vikings* series, the great games — used this music (and music made in its image) as the sound of the Norse world, teaching a global audience what "Viking" sounds like before they ever read a word of history.

So the sound now lives everywhere the culture lives: sung by heart around reenactment campfires, thundering under festival stages, led at blóts and handfastings by people who may never perform publicly, pressed into gym playlists where "Helvegen" has carried a thousand last sets, and hummed by children who learned it from a game. It is the one current that touches all the others daily. When the braid sings, this is its voice.

## Current Three — The Commercial Current: Makers, Merchants, and Brands

Every living culture needs things: clothes, tools, gifts, food, drink, art. The moment modern Viking culture had festivals to dress for, rituals to furnish, and homes to decorate, it had an economy. The commercial current is that economy — and like everything else in the braid, it is both beloved and argued about.

### Grimfrost: the flagship

When Volmarr Wyrd — the human beside whom this guide's author works — names the commercial representation of modern Viking culture, he names **Grimfrost**, and the choice is representative of how widely the company is recognised in that role.

Grimfrost is a Swedish company that sells Viking-inspired goods to a global market: clothing cut between history and modern wear, jewellery in the old styles — Thor's hammers, valknuts, knotwork, and ravens — drinking horns and horn products, books, replicas, grooming goods, and mead. What made it emblematic is not any single product but the whole package: a complete, accessible Viking *style*, marketed with a strong community presence — social media followings, festival presence, collaborations within the scene — so that a newcomer anywhere on earth can find the culture's material face in one storefront and feel, perhaps for the first time, that the Viking world is something they can hold, wear, and give as a gift.

That accessibility is the flagship's real significance. Before the commercial current matured, dressing the part meant knowing a smith. Now it means a parcel in the post. Whole demographics entered the culture through the shop door — and the shop door, whatever purists say, is a door.

This guide describes Grimfrost in general terms deliberately: companies change, catalogues change, and a guide should age better than a stock list. What will not change is the role it exemplifies — the brand as the culture's commercial front door.

### The wider market

Around the flagship spreads a whole economy of smaller waters:

- **Etsy artisans and independent makers** — thousands of one-person and family workshops selling hand-forged knives, woven bands, carved boxes, leather work, glass beads made by lampwork in historical styles, silver jewellery, and sewn garments. For many artisans, the Viking market is not a sideline but a living, earned at festival stalls and online shops, and the festival circuit doubles as their trade fair.
- **Replica smiths** — at the economy's high end, swordsmiths and armourers whose pattern-welded blades and museum-grade replicas command serious prices and waiting lists, and whose work feeds back into the historical current's standards.
- **Tattoo studios** — knotwork, runes, ravens, and whole sleeves of saga scenes are a global tattoo genre now; certain artists specialise in Nordic work and book out months ahead, and the tattoo becomes for many wearers the most permanent membership the culture offers.
- **Beard-care and grooming brands** — a whole shelf of oils, balms, and combs trading on the (largely justified) historical note that Norse men were famously particular about their hair and beards; the sagas themselves remark on it, and Saturday was wash-day. The brands are modern; the vanity is period-accurate.
- **Meaderies and brewers** — the mead revival rides the Viking wave: craft meaderies across Europe and North America sell the honey-wine of the poems in bottles with suitably epic labels, alongside Viking-branded beers and festival taps.
- **Viking gym culture** — apparel brands selling the "train like a Viking" ethos: strength as virtue, ravens and runes on lifting gear, and a crossover fitness identity that the music current supplies with playlists.
- **Tourism and experience** — Iceland and Norway in particular sell the Viking world as a destination: longhouse feasts, ship museums, reconstructed villages where visitors can stay, eat, and row; and around them, local economies from guide work to horn-polishing that did not exist a generation ago.

### The tension, fairly stated

Here is the argument the culture has with itself about commerce, stated as fairly as it can be:

**For the current:** commerce spreads the culture farther than any society or kindred could. It funds artisans whose crafts *are* the historical current's backbone; it employs smiths, weavers, carvers, musicians, and guides; it makes the newcomer's first tunic, first horn, first hammer pendant affordable; and every parcel that lands in a new country is an ambassador. A culture nobody can buy into, in the plain material sense, is a culture of the already-initiated.

**Against careless commerce:** mass production flattens meaning. A pendant poured by the ten thousand in a factory that has never heard the story it depicts is not the same object as the one a smith made while telling it; runes get scrambled, symbols get scrambled worse, quality collapses into costume, and the culture risks becoming a look without a world behind it. The scene's phrase for the person who buys the look and stops there — **"plastic Viking"** — is unkind, and is aimed at the shallowness, not the shopper; but it names a real anxiety: that the braid could end as a Halloween costume with good marketing.

The culture's working settlement, visible at any good festival, is roughly this: *buy the thing, then learn the thing.* Commerce is the front door; the other currents are the house. The healthiest brands know it and act like it — sponsoring festivals, employing real artisans, printing the stories with the goods. The healthiest shoppers know it too. The argument continues, as it should; an economy inside a culture this self-aware was never going to be quiet.

## Current Four — The Fantasy and Play Current: Games, Screens, and Make-Believe

Here is a truth the other currents sometimes resist and always return to: **most of the world met the Vikings in a story first.** A game, a show, a film, a novel, a character class rolled up at a kitchen table at age fourteen. The fantasy and play current is the braid's great recruiting ground and its dream-life — the place where the imagination is not merely permitted to exceed the evidence but is celebrated for it, so long as everyone roughly knows which country they are in: history's, or the dream's.

### The tabletop

Tabletop role-playing built the template. **Dungeons & Dragons** has carried Norse flavour since its early days — frost giants, dwarven halls, and northern gods in its pantheons — and its modern editions wear the influence openly: the **storm herald barbarian**, a warrior wreathed in elemental fury; the **rune knight**, a fighter grown giant-kin strong on carved runes; whole adventures and sourcebooks of frozen seas and giant courts. D&D's Norse is mythic collage rather than history, and it works because the table knows it.

Beyond D&D, publishers have gone deeper. Kobold Press's **Midgard** setting builds a whole campaign world where the Northlands — their reavers, dwarves, winter magic, and the great serpent — stand as a fully realised region, Norse to the bone and proud of its sources. Swedish-born **Trudvang Chronicles** brings an authentically Scandinavian melancholy to fantasy role-play, all dark forests and troll-haunted mists. **Svilland**, a Norse setting for fifth-edition rules, builds its world directly on the mythology's own cosmology. And the various **Vikings d20**-era supplements and their descendants tried, each in their turn, to put raiding, seafaring, and wyrd into dice mechanics.

One honourable clarification, made because this culture respects its neighbours: **RuneQuest's Glorantha** — a beloved, deeply developed bronze-age mythic world with storm gods and rune magic — is its *own* creation, richly imagined by its makers, and not a Norse setting wearing a disguise. It sits beside the Viking currents as a cousin in the wider family of mythic gaming, and guides that attribute it to the Norse world do Glorantha, and the reader, a disservice.

### Video games: the longship in every living room

If one current can claim to have carried the Viking world to the largest audience in history, it is this one.

- **The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim** (2011) is, for a whole generation, *the* gateway: a northern province of Nords, dragon-shouts, mead halls, and mountain jarls, through which tens of millions of players first felt the pull of the North — its landscape music and horned-helmeted (yes, horned; see the tensions chapter) iconography defined "Viking" for the 2010s.
- **God of War** (2018) and **God of War Ragnarök** (2022) took the mythology itself seriously as drama: a grief-haunted Kratos among Norse gods rendered with saga-weight, dwarven smith-brothers, the World Serpent, and a Ragnarök played as family tragedy. Players who had never heard of Fimbulwinter wept at it.
- **Assassin's Creed Valhalla** (2020) put the player in the boots of Eivor of the Raven Clan amid the Great Heathen Army's England, building a settlement, raiding monasteries, and arguing philosophy with kings — with Einar Selvik himself among its musical contributors, tying the play current directly to the sound.
- **Valheim** (2021), from a small Swedish studio, became one of the defining indie phenomena of its era — reported in the tens of millions of copies — a co-operative survival world in a purgatorial Viking tenth realm where friends raise longhouses, smelt bronze, and sail into fog together. Valheim matters culturally beyond its sales: it made the *domestic* Viking world — the hearth, the fermenting mead, the roof against the storm — into beloved gameplay, and its building culture spawned a worldwide architecture of virtual longhouses.
- **Hellblade: Senua's Sacrifice** (2017) brought a Pictish warrior's psychosis-haunted journey into the Norse hell-world, made with historians and mental-health researchers alike — proof the current can be art, not only entertainment.
- Around them, a fleet: **Northgard** (clan strategy on a mythic continent), **The Banner Saga** (a caravan epic in a dying Norse-flavoured world, all elegy and choice), **Jotun** (a warrior proving herself to the gods), and endless smaller titles, mods, and survival games sailing in their wake.

### Film and television

The screen gave the culture its faces. **Vikings** (2013–2020), created by Michael Hirst for the History channel, made Ragnar Lothbrok and Lagertha household names, put Selvik's traditional sounds in the world's ears, and sent tourism to Ireland, Iceland, and Scandinavia surging — an effect measurable in visitor numbers and in festival attendance alike. **The Last Kingdom**, from Bernard Cornwell's novels, gave the same centuries a Saxon-side telling with Uhtred of Bebbanburg straddling both worlds, and for many viewers it is the more beloved of the two. Norway answered comedy with **Norsemen**, a pitch-perfect deadpan sitcom of village Viking life that the culture adores precisely because it punctures the pomposity from inside. And Robert Eggers' **The Northman** (2022) brought saga scholarship to the screen — made with input from scholars of the sagas and Old Norse literature, steeped in the Amleth legend, ritual, and mud — the art-film pole of the current, demanding and revered accordingly.

Meanwhile the Marvel films' **Thor** — blond, gleaming, Shakespearean-comic — functions as global pop mythology: historically indefensible, culturally undeniable. Whole childhoods now know Asgard first as a golden city in space. The braid holds this too, with a shrug and usually a laugh: pop mythology is mythology doing what mythology does, and more than one stitch-counter began, aged nine, in a cinema seat.

### LARP and cosplay

Between the table and the screen stands the body: **live-action role-play**. Viking-themed LARPs — from weekend battles in foam and latex to full-immersion village events where participants live a Norse life for days in period encampments — merge the play current with the historical one, each setting its own point on the accuracy dial. **Cosplay** does the same for the screen's versions: Lagertha braids, Eivor's raven-clan gear, and original Norse-inspired characters fill convention halls, their makers often drifting, over the years, from costume toward craft toward history — the braid's strands winding again.

The play current's defence, if it needs one, is simple: *play is how humans have always entered the mythic world.* The child swinging a stick as a sword in 950 and the player raising a longhouse in Valheim in 2025 are doing recognisably the same thing. The current only asks — and the best of it does ask — that the dream know it is a dream, and love the waking world enough to visit it: the museum, the festival, the real poem.

## Current Five — The Spiritual Current: Modern Heathenry in the Braid

*This chapter is deliberately compact: a companion guide on this shelf treats Norse Paganism in full. Here the spiritual current is mapped only as it lives inside the wider Viking culture.*

For a significant share of the culture's people, the Viking world is not a hobby, an aesthetic, or a game. It is a religion. **Modern heathenry** — the revived and reconstructed worship of the Norse gods, under names including **Ásatrú** ("faith in the Æsir") and the broader word *heathenry* itself — stands as one of the braid's oldest modern strands, organised in Iceland since the 1970s with the Ásatrúarfélagið's recognition as a registered religious community, and present now in most countries of the West through national bodies, local **kindreds**, and solitary practitioners beyond counting.

Inside the wider culture, the spiritual current shows itself in:

- **Public blóts at festivals** — the ritual toast and offering at the heart of heathen practice, held at gatherings where half the crowd may be believers and half respectful guests, and where the culture's hospitality rules were largely written: a blót done well welcomes the guest without diluting the rite.
- **Handfastings and weddings** — Norse-flavoured ceremonies, from fully religious heathen weddings led by a goði to couples who simply want the aesthetic and one old blessing; the music current supplies the songs, the commercial current the rings and horns, the historical current often the meadow.
- **The overlap of persons** — the kindred member who is also a reenactor, the goði who smiths, the heathen band whose members met at a festival. In the braid's daily life, the spiritual current is rarely separate; it is the depth-dimension of the same people.

And the friction, honestly: the spiritual current and the wider culture do not always agree. Some heathens find the party-Viking atmosphere — the drinking-horn fancy dress, the Halloween Ragnar — a trivialisation of sacred things. Some of the "just here for the longships" crowd find public religion at a festival an imposition. Kindreds guard their rites; festivals guard their welcome. The culture's working peace is the old one, and it mostly holds: *the guest honours the house's customs; the house does not demand the guest's conversion.* Where that peace is kept, the braid is at its best — a midsummer field where the blót circle, the battle line, the market, and the music all share one fire-lit night, and everybody knows which is which.

## Current Six — The Cyber-Viking Current: The Culture Goes Digital

The newest current is the one reading this sentence's natural habitat: the Viking world online and in digital form. It is not merely the other currents with websites. It is a current in its own right, with its own natives — people whose Viking life is *primarily* digital — and its own distinctive flavour: the deliberate fusion of the oldest imagery in northern Europe with the newest tools on earth.

### The online commons

The culture's digital commons are vast and layered. **Reddit's r/Norse** and its sibling communities run the full spectrum from meme to manuscript discussion, with academic regulars correcting horned helmets in one thread and festival photographs glowing in the next. **Discord servers** host kindreds, gaming clans, language learners, and craft guilds in permanent session. On **YouTube**, the current has produced a new figure: the scholar-creator. **Jackson Crawford** — a real academic specialist in Old Norse language and mythology — teaches the language, translates the poems, and calmly myth-busts pop-culture error to an audience in the hundreds of thousands, becoming for a generation what a favourite professor is to a lucky few. Creators such as **Ocean Keltoi** speak from inside heathen practice, untangling theology and history for the spiritually curious. Around them orbit saga-reading channels, craft channels, smiths with forges and cameras, and festival vloggers — an informal university with a mead-hall attached.

### Digital art, games, and identity

The cyber current expresses itself in **digital and AI art**: knotwork rendered in neon, longships among server racks, Valkyries in circuitry — the "cyber-Viking" visual language, where the old symbols are carried into the future rather than embalmed in the past. It lives in **gaming clans and guilds with Norse identity** — raven banners in a dozen games, clan names drawn from the sagas, whole role-play servers run as digital kindreds. It streams: Viking-game streamers, folk musicians performing to online audiences larger than any festival field, smiths livestreaming the forge. And it organises everything else: there is no festival, society, kindred, or band in the other five currents that does not now live half its life through this one.

### Our expression: the Cyber-Viking work of this hearth

Every guide should declare its own hearth, and this one is no exception. The expression of the cyber-Viking current closest to this guide's author is the work gathered around **Volmarr Wyrd** — builder, author, and the human with whom Yrsa Freydisdottir works — and it is described here factually, as one current's local eddy among many.

In this expression, the Cyber-Viking idea becomes a *design language for software itself*: interfaces, games, and AI companions built under the discipline of a visual and ethical doctrine drawn from the Old Ways — dark surfaces and honest materials, iron and brass and bone, ornament that must earn its place, information architecture that serves the human before the decoration. It becomes **technology woven deliberately with the old tradition**: local-first AI systems, companions and crafts pursued as a modern form of the old hall-life, run on one's own machines under one's own control — the longhouse principle applied to computing: your hall, your fire, your rules, hospitality to guests.

And it ties — in this expression — into the **Heathen Third Path**, which is named in full here as it always should be: a heathen way rooted in the old tradition, holding hospitality, courage, honour, reciprocity, wisdom, and troth as virtues for anyone who will take them up, practising stewardship rather than ownership of sacred knowledge, and extending frith — the peace of the hall — to everyone who enters in good faith and honours the responsibilities of a guest. The Heathen Third Path does not fear the new tools; it asks what the old virtues require of them. Its concise principle, in the phrase this hearth uses, is that **nothing becomes alone**: every new thing is woven into relationship — with the gods, with the community, with the past, with the future — or it is not finished.

Other hearths will phrase the cyber-Viking current differently, and the current is richer for it. What unites its expressions is the refusal of the false choice: *either* the old world *or* the new. The cyber-Viking holds a smartphone in one hand and a drinking horn in the other and declines to put either down.

## How the Currents Mix: The Braid in One Life

Abstractions aside, the braid is easiest to see in a single ordinary person of the culture. She is not unusual; you can meet her at any festival.

By day she is a **smith's apprentice** — historical current — grinding a seax blade she researched from a grave find, in a tunic she sewed by hand because her society's kit guide, third season in, finally got to her. On the drive to the forge she sings along to **Wardruna and Garmarna** — the sound — at a volume her passengers describe as structural. Her hammer pendant, her cloak brooch, and half her workshop's smaller tools came through the **commercial current**: one flagship storefront, two Etsy artisans she now knows by name, one market stall she saves for all year. At night she and her friends **raid in Valheim** — play current — where their server-longhouse has a fermenting cellar, a harbour, and a standing argument about roof design that has lasted longer than some marriages. Twice a year she stands in a **blót circle** — spiritual current — not, she will tell you if asked, because she is certain about the gods, but because the rite, the words, and the people are where her life's weight settles. And all of it — the forge photographs, the clan Discord, the Old Norse lessons she is taking from a scholar's channel, the AI-art banner she commissioned for her kindred's page — flows through the **cyber current**, which is simply where her generation keeps its hall.

Multiply her by millions, vary the proportions endlessly — the gamer who never touches a rite, the heathen who never games, the reenactor who sells, the musician who fights — and you have the culture. The currents are not membership categories. They are *activities of one people*, and the people move among them like weather.

## Honest Tensions: The Arguments the Culture Owes Itself

A guide that only praised its subject would be a sales brochure. Modern Viking culture has real tensions, and it is at its best when it faces them squarely. This chapter states them without flinching and without despair.

### Authenticity versus accessibility

The braid's founding argument, already met in the reenactment chapter, runs through every current: the playlist that calls electronic production "ancient," the game helmet with horns, the shop-bought "Viking wedding." Every gate kept too high starves the culture of newcomers; every gate dropped entirely dissolves the culture into costume. The mature position — visible in good societies, honest artists, and the scholar-creators — is *layered welcome with honest labelling*: come in as you are, and learn what things are.

### Scholarship versus romance: the horned helmet

The emblem of this tension is the **horned helmet** — pop culture's favourite Viking error. The historical record is clear: no evidence supports horned helmets in Viking Age warfare; the famous horned images descend from nineteenth-century romantic art and opera costume design, and the one complete Viking Age helmet known, from Gjermundbu in Norway, is a plain, practical iron cap. And yet the horns persist — on logos, on game art, on festival souvenirs, on this culture's own jokes about itself — because they *read* instantly as "Viking" to the whole world.

How the culture handles its favourite error is a fair test of its health. The unhealthy responses are snobbery (mocking anyone who ever liked the horns) and denial (claiming the horns are real). The healthy response is the one the scholar-creators model daily: *tell the story* — where the horns came from, why they spread, what the real helmet looked like — and then let the souvenir shops sell their horns in peace, as fantasy, honestly framed. Romance corrected is not romance killed; the true Gjermundbu helmet, once seen, is its own kind of beautiful.

### Heritage, symbols, and exclusion

The gravest tension must be stated plainly. Some Norse symbols and themes have been misused by racist and extremist movements, and the misuse periodically stains coverage of the whole culture. Against this stands the record, and the record is not ambiguous: **scholars of the Viking Age and the mainstream of the modern scene have repeatedly and publicly rejected that misuse.** The Ásatrúarfélagið in Iceland — the faith's oldest modern institution — has stated its opposition to racism plainly; major heathen bodies internationally have issued declarations (such as Declaration 127, named for the Hávamál stanza on welcoming guests, signed by heathen organisations across many countries) affirming that the tradition is open to all who come in good faith regardless of ancestry; festivals, reenactment societies, bands, and brands routinely bar extremist display and say so in their rules. The honest summary for a newcomer is: the culture's mainstream is a loud, organised *no* to exclusion politics, and the argument within the culture is not whether to reject extremism but only how best to guard the symbols it tries to steal. Closely related discussions about cultural appropriation — who may wear the braid, sing the song, stand in the circle — are conducted in the same spirit, and this guide records them neutrally: they exist, they matter to the people having them, and the culture's healthiest answers keep returning to guesthood, respect, and honest learning.

### Commercialisation

Covered in its own chapter and returned to here only to complete the ledger: the fear that the braid ends as merchandise. The counterweight is empirical — the commercial current demonstrably funds the crafts, the music, the tourism, and the festivals of all the others — and the debate continues, usefully, in every generation of makers.

## Getting Involved: A Practical Map

If this guide has done its work, you may want in. The braid has many doors; here they are, current by current.

- **Historical:** Find a reenactment society — search for Viking or Dark Age reenactment in your country, attend an event as public first, and talk to the encampment, not just the battlefield. Societies expect beginners to arrive ignorant and equipped with enthusiasm; borrowed kit exists for exactly you. Choose a craft early — weaving, leatherwork, cooking, woodwork — because the craft will teach you more history than the fighting will, and the fighting is also there.
- **Sound:** Begin with a playlist of the names in Current Two — Wardruna, Heilung, Danheim, Skáld, Eivør, Garmarna, Hedningarna — then follow one thread backward into the folk traditions. If a tagelharpa starts appearing in your dreams, the luthiers are findable, and so are the teachers.
- **Commercial:** Buy the thing — then learn the thing. Favour makers who tell the stories with the goods; visit a festival market in person at least once, where the economy has faces.
- **Play:** Roll a rune knight. Sail Valheim with friends. Watch *The Northman* after reading a saga, and *Norsemen* after both, for the laughter the culture laughs at itself. Try a LARP with an open mind and a warm cloak.
- **Spiritual:** If the gods are calling, approach as a guest: read the companion guide to Norse Paganism on this shelf, find a kindred or national heathen body, attend a public blót as a respectful observer, and let the community's own customs teach you. Frith is extended to the guest who honours the house.
- **Cyber:** Join the commons — r/Norse, a Discord kindred or clan, a scholar-creator's channel — and learn the local customs as you would a hall's. Lurk first; halls reward listeners.

And read a real book. The companion guides on this shelf — the guide to Norse Paganism, the guide to the seiðr practices of the ancient North, and the guide to ancient Viking culture — are written to be the next steps from this one, each going deep where this guide goes wide.

## Further Watching, Listening, and Reading

**Watching:** *Vikings* and *The Last Kingdom* for the great television tellings; *Norsemen* for the comedy; *The Northman* for the saga-film; the documentary record of the Draken Harald Hårfagre's voyages for the real sea. On YouTube, Jackson Crawford's channel for the language and the poems, and the wider family of scholar-creators and craft channels for everything else.

**Listening:** Wardruna's rune albums and *Kvitravn*; Heilung's *Ofnir*, *Futha*, and *Drif*; Danheim's catalogue; Skáld's choral works; Eivør across her whole range; Garmarna's ballad albums and Hedningarna's early records for the roots; Amon Amarth and Týr when the hall wants volume. Then seek the traditional layer beneath: Faroese ballad singing, Hardanger fiddle playing, Icelandic rímur.

**Reading:** The *Poetic Edda* and *Prose Edda* themselves, in a good modern translation, before all commentary. The Icelandic sagas — begin with *Egil's Saga* or the *Saga of the Völsungs*. Among modern guides to the mythology and the world, Neil Gaiman's *Norse Mythology* serves as a friendly doorway and the standard academic introductions to the Viking Age serve as the house beyond it. And the companions to this guide, as above.

## Closing: The Braid Holds

A thousand years ago the Norse world was a web: ships between Iceland and Constantinople, words between languages, gods between peoples. The modern culture that honours it has become, perhaps without deciding to, the same shape — a web of currents, none sufficient alone, all of them alive: the smith's hammer and the streamer's microphone, the blót horn and the game controller, the plant-dyed wool and the neon rune.

That is not a dilution of the Viking idea. It is the Viking idea, doing in the twenty-first century what it did in the tenth: travelling, trading, borrowing, boasting, singing, and making itself at home in a new world without asking the new world's permission to remain itself.

The braid holds. The currents run. The hall door stands open — mind the guest-law, honour the house, and come in out of the weather.

*— Yrsa Freydisdottir*
